\section*{Abstract} 
%
\textbf{
Single-cyclist crashes remain a major issue for the field of cycling safety.
%
However, the underlying physics and conditions that cause crashes are still unclear.
%
The lack of clear crash categories hampers the research of specific mechanisms and situations.
%
Here we develop a single-cyclist crash classification consistent with the underlying laws of physics to explain crash mechanisms.
%
We reviewed existing classifications, extracted the hypothesised crash causes, and evaluated against established bicycle dynamics.
%
The proposed crash classification works as a sequential framework to assess the circumstances and conditions, consisting of five classes: pre-crash manoeuvre, nature of the perturbation, crash mechanisms, recoverability, and crash motion.
%
The classes are related by causality.
%
The proposed class for crash mechanisms comprises balance control, contact forces, load transfer, rider-bicycle interface, and tyre friction.
%
The class for crash motions includes collision ejection, pitch-over, and roll-over.
%
These definitions serve to classify single-cyclist crashes in a standardised methodology.
%
A standardised methodology allows consistent classifications and information throughout different studies, improving the quality of the research by reducing ambiguities.
%
We tested the classification through a questionnaire sent to 17 participants who had to classify ten single-cyclist crash videos each.
%
The level of agreement ranged from 54\% to 83\% among the classes.
%
These results support the improved objectivity on the process of classifying single-cyclist crashes.
}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec: introduction}
% 
In Europe, cycling is, and in a growing number of regions, becoming a major alternative to motor vehicle use.
%
To promote bicycle usage, the risks need to be minimised~\cite{Eur24}.
%
Furthermore, if the risks are not reduced, bicycle usage may decrease~\cite{Gu19}.
%
Unlike bicycles, issues in road safety have been seriously improved for cars, which has translated into a decrease in car crashes.
%
A key factor in improved car safety has been the driving assistance systems, such as stability control, anti-lock braking systems, and collision avoidance systems, among others~\cite{Ben14}.
%
With such systems, the cognitive load for the driver has been reduced, giving a wider margin of error, therefore, decreasing the number of car crashes.
%
In contrast, bicycles have seen minimal safety-technology evolution during the same period.
%
The major evolution has been electric pedal assistance~\cite{Kap22}, where safety implications are still inconclusive~\cite{Bai13, Hau16, Sch18a, Sch20}.
%
It is expected that bicycle safety technology may be able to reduce crash occurrences, similar to car driving assistances. 
%
However, the first step in mitigating single-cyclist crashes with technology is to develop a causal and mechanistic understanding of the crashes.
%%


Primarily, single-cyclist crashes are events where no other road user is involved~\cite{Weg24}.
%
These stand out for their large share of all bicycle crashes, accounting for 80\% of cyclist-related hospital admissions in the Netherlands~\cite{Boe17}.
%
These numbers are reflected in other countries: 50\% to 85\% in Finland~\cite{Utr23}, 68\% in Sweden~\cite{Ohl19}, \textgreater50\% in Australia~\cite{Bou13}, 31\% in Belgium~\cite{Van16}, and 29\% in Ireland~\cite{Gil21a}.
%
Most of these statistics come from hospital records and police reports.
%%


In the Netherlands, the risk of death in single-cyclist crashes increased by 7\% in the period from 1996 to 2014~\cite{Sch17}. 
%
While from 2013 to 2023, car passenger fatalities have decreased by 17 \% in the Netherlands~\cite{Cen24, Sti25}.
%
This trend is also present in other European countries, showing a reduction in other modes of transportation fatalities, but maintaining either a constant or an increasing number of bicycle fatalities~\cite{Eur24}.
%
As a result, the share of bicycle fatalities compared to total road fatalities increases.
%%


A single-cyclist crash is an interplay of three factors: vehicle, human, and environment~\cite{Fel76, Zha22}.
%
From these, environmental factors, i.e. infrastructure design and interaction with other road users, have been thoroughly assessed and improved in the last decade~\cite{Sch17a}.
%
Conversely, the human is known to be an important factor~\cite{Orm09}, but the rider's role in the outcome of the event is not yet fully understood.
%
Furthermore, the role of the bicycle has not been properly assessed, since past data is not tied back to the interplay of factors and dynamics between the rider, bicycle and environment, which are not adequately described for single-cyclist crashes.
%
As the Dutch Institute for Road Safety Research (SWOV) states, \textit{`...whether the vehicle features of bicycles play a role in crash occurrence is hard to determine, and even if this role is determined, it is not systematically registered...'}~\cite{Sti23}.
%
Understanding crash dynamics and failure mechanisms is crucial to develop safer conditions for cycling.
%
To advance bicycle safety, we need to accurately classify crashes so that we can obtain basic metrics and outcomes like the number, type, and circumstances under which crashes occur.
%%


Current classification systems lead to ambiguous, and often inconsistent, analysis of common factors and hypothesised mechanisms, such as slippery road conditions, visibility status, infrastructure concerns, among others~\cite{Utr23}.
%
While these studies are valuable, they often overlook the particular dynamics of the bicycle and human biomechanics.
%
It is known that human control of the system requires the rider sensing and actuating to move from one state to another.
%
Here, dynamics govern whether and how forces acting on the system can transition the state.
%
Thus, it is needed to understand the forces acting on the bicycle, the motion state of the rider-bicycle system, and the control authority that the human is capable of.
%
Therefore, the major objective in the pursuit of an improved road safety, is to understand bicycle crash dynamics, where more detailed information is required~\cite{Haw13, Møl21}.
%%


To gather more accurate crash information, it is first necessary to objectively identify the individual crash features and cluster them by their underlying dynamics.
%
In this regard, biomechanics and vehicle dynamics studies require objective and standardised information to describe what is happening just before and during the crash.
%
With this information, it is possible to objectively understand the causality and have information to intervene or guide the design towards bicycles less susceptible to crashes.
%%


The lack of objectivity is a source of bias in the assessment of crash causes~\cite{Shi19}.
%
In fact, Shinar states \textit{`Crash cause is similar to beauty: it is in the eye of the beholder'}.
%
This evidences that the field of single-cyclist crashes lacks objective consensus.
%
Therefore, objective and mechanistic classification criteria are needed.
%%


Since single-cyclist crash classifications are neither standardised nor objectively assessed, we expose a knowledge gap regarding how crash information is gathered.
%
Therefore, we aim to develop objective and unambiguous criteria that span the diversity and causality of single-cyclist crashes.
%
To this end, we review the existing classifications and contrast them with bicycle dynamics.
%
As a result, we present a standard approach to classify bicycle crashes with precise and objective concepts.
%%

\subsection{Definitions} \label{subsec: terminology}
%
In the field of single-cyclist crashes, there is no clear agreement on the terminology used for similar concepts.
%
Therefore, we develop the terminology used throughout our work based on the concepts found in the literature.
%%
\begin{description}
    %
    \item [Single-track vehicle:] vehicles which leave a single road track as it moves forward. 
    %
    Bicycles and motorcycles are examples of these, and share the main characteristics as dynamical systems, which makes them similar for studies~\cite{Lim06}.
    %
    In its analytical study, both are usually assumed as four-body wheeled vehicles, with roll, pitch, yaw, steer, and wheel rotation as angular degrees of freedom, plus the spatial position.
    %
    Both vehicles experience similar crash configurations~\cite{Haw13, Ken22, Llo16}, including tyre skidding, inability to control the system through the steering, excessive pitch rotation, among others, discussed later in this paper.
    %
    The main differences are the mass, the working speed range, and the main power source.
    %%
    

    \item [Rider:] a human operator who senses and can control themselves, and other subsystems, a bicycle, for instance, within a range of actuation and control due to strength and reaction time limitations.
    

    \item [Bicycle:] a two-wheeled human-powered single-track vehicle, which is controlled by the rider through the pedals, handlebar, and brakes as the control interfaces.
    %
    According to the European regulations, pedal cycles with pedal assistance of less than or equal to 250 [W], and that cut off before 25 [km/h] are considered bicycles and not motorcycles~\cite{Eur13}.
    %
    It is studied as a system on its own, composed of wheels, front and rear frames, pedals, saddle, handlebars, and brakes.
    %%
    

    \item [Rider-Bicycle System:] the closed loop of a controlled bicycle~\cite{Hor95}.
    %
    The bicycle and the rider are coupled together through the physical interfaces, primarily pedals, saddle, and handlebars.
    %
    Thus, the rider controls the lateral balance of the bicycle mainly through handlebars steering, and also with the contribution of hips, pedals, and legs.
    %
    Similarly, for forward acceleration and deceleration, the rider relies on pedalling and braking.
    %%
    
    
    \item [Perturbation:] an undesired signal that cause the system to deviate from its intended behaviour.
    %
    This definition is in alignment with the concept of `disturbance' employed by~\cite{Oga10a}.
    %
    An active perturbation is a controlled signal from an external source, such as another vehicle colliding with the system.
    %
    A passive perturbation is a non-controlled outside influence.
    %%
    

    \item [Slip:] refers to one of the main characteristics of tyres, where it can be longitudinal, lateral, or spin~\cite{Pac06}.
    %
    This phenomenon primarily occurs due to tyre deformation, where the reaction force from the tyre-ground friction interface acts through the tyre carcass, generating a gap between the orientation and the actual motion.
    %
    The angle between the lateral and forward velocity of the wheel is known as `slip angle'~\cite{Pac06}.
    %%
    

    \item [Skidding:] unlike slip, skidding is relative motion that exceeds the grip limits of the tyre, causing the tyre to slide.
    %
    We adopt the term `skidding' as it is specifically used in the field of vehicle-dynamics to describe the loss of control due to achieving the maximum lateral force that the tyre can handle \cite{Kar13}.
    %%
    

    \item [Crash:] a sudden and unintended situation that disrupts the normal and controlled motion of the rider-bicycle system, causing the rider to fall from the bicycle.
    %
    Wienen et al. define accident as \textit{`... an undesired and unplanned event that results in a loss, damage or injury'}~\cite{Wie18}.
    %
    Whereas the definition from the American National Standard is \textit{`... an unstabilized situation which includes at least one harmful event'}~\cite{Ame17a}. 
    %
    Although the word accident is usually employed in a broader assessment of the event, its usage is discouraged due to the implication of a preventable event~\cite{Dav01}.
    %
    As shown, the definitions of a crash in the literature do not fit properly the events to which bicycles are susceptible. 
    %%
    
    
    \item [Single-Vehicle Crash:] a crash where no other road user is involved.
    %
    However, other road users can still indirectly influence the rider since the human senses its environment through vision, contact, smell and hearing.
    %
    For example, a cyclist sees a pedestrian and needs to swerve to avoid them, resulting in a crash by running off the road.
    %
    Therefore, single-vehicle crashes can include other road users exerting a non-contact influence on the rider~\cite{Orm09}.
    %%
    
    
    \item [Single-Cyclist Crash:] as previously detailed, this refers to a crash where other road users do not have a direct influence over the control of the system.
    %
    In the literature, a variety of names are given: single-bicycle crash~\cite{Alg22, Bec19, Boe17, Gil23a, Møl21, Ohl19, Sch12, Sch15, Sch17, Sch18a, Twi13, Utr20, Utr23, Utr23a, Van16}, single-actor bicycle crash~\cite{Ali23}, single-cyclist crash~\cite{Gil21, Gil21a}, among others~\cite{Bou13, Bou16, Her18, Sch21}.
    %
    On the one hand, the expression `single-bicycle crash', even being the most common, neglects the rider, making reference to a problem only oriented on the bicycle.
    %
    On the other hand, single-actor bicycle crashes assume that there are no other actors involved in the event, which neglects the indirect effects of other road users.
    %
    Furthermore, any other denomination that includes the word `accident' is discouraged as previously mentioned.
    %
    Therefore, for this work, we adopt the term \textit{single-cyclist crash}, due to its reference to the rider and the bicycle as the main actors in the event, allowing other indirect influences.
    %%
\end{description}


\subsubsection{Single-cyclist crash description in detail} \label{subsec: crash anatomy}
%
A single-cyclist crash is the result of the rider losing control of the bicycle, which is the inability of the user to manoeuvre the vehicle to a desired state.
%
\cite{Ful05} describes, in his task-capability interface model, the loss of control as a relation between task demand and rider capability.
%
When the task demand surpasses rider's capability, the rider loses control and the crash occurs.
%
This model considers physical characteristics and knowledge of the rider on what to do under determined circumstances to define the boundaries of their capability.
%
However, this capacity is also affected by other factors related to the circumstance, such as awareness, emotion, and fatigue, among others.
%
Conversely, task-demanding elements encompass travelling speed, environmental factors, such as road characteristics and other road users.
%
Of these factors, the rider has direct control of the travelling speed, achieving a balance between rider capacity and their awareness of the task.
%
However, a major difference between car drivers and bicycle riders is that the latter has an extra task: maintaining lateral balance.
%
While car drivers can reduce task demand by decreasing speed, for bicycle riders, decreasing the speed can increase stabilisation requirements, and therefore, increasing task demand \cite{Koo11, Sch13, Vla15}.
%
In general, the theory shows an almost constant gap between driver capability and changing task demands.
%%


When task demands surpass a rider's capability, a critical scenario becomes a loss of control and a possible crash, depending on the extent of the loss of control.
%
\cite{Gil23a} characterises the level of loss of control as minor, substantial, and complete.
%
Minor is when the wheel gets caught on a track, loses grip, or even the system experiences a disturbance but the rider is able to recover and continue their motion.
%
Substantial considers when the rider requires to stop or dismount the bicycle to regain balance.
%
Finally, complete loss of control is when the result is the cyclist falling from the bicycle.
%%


When the critical scenario becomes a complete loss of control, it leads to a crash as defined in Section \ref{subsec: terminology}.
%
In order to understand and mitigate crashes, it becomes vital to analyse bicycle crash dynamics.
%
Crash dynamics is the study of the forces, energy transfer, and motion during the loss of control in the rider-bicycle system.
%
It encompasses the analysis of how the vehicle and its occupant respond to impacts, considering factors like speed, mass, and direction.
%
The analysis of crash dynamics is typically divided into three distinct phases: pre-crash, crash, and post-crash motion \cite{Par01}.
%%


The pre-crash phase begins with a perturbation and ends when the rider no longer has the control authority to recover.
%
During this phase, the system's kinetic and potential energy, along with rider control, determine the trajectory of the bicycle.
%
In this stage, energy is rapidly and uncontrolledly redistributed, modifying the behaviour of the system.
%%


The crash phase is composed of the uncontrolled motions as a result of the crash mechanisms, together with protective responses from the rider.
%
Then, the post-crash is typically brief and not well-defined in single-cyclist crashes, due to the inherent instability of the bicycle, it will tend to rest immediately after the crash itself.
%
Therefore, post-crash is defined by the moment the bicycle contacts the ground with components other than the wheels \cite{Llo16}.
%%


Crash causes have been assessed for more than 10 years.
%
However, most of them lack a carefully mechanistic assessment.
%
Previous classification attempts tend to focus on the infrastructure as environmental factors, while overlooking the biomechanics of the rider, and simplifying the importance bicycle dynamics.
%
From single-track vehicle dynamics, the critical aspects for bicycle stability and control concerns can be identified.
%
Using these critical aspects to explain crashes, enables a more objective evaluation of the events.
%
Moreover, rigorously assessing the origin of the crash leads to a clear understanding of the mechanisms and measures that can be taken to prevent crashes.
%%

\subsection{Literature review}
%
\subsubsection{Single-cyclist crash classifications} \label{subsec: classifications}
%
Currently, studies on single-cyclist crashes are based on the available data, which is mainly descriptive from hospital, police, and insurance reports.
%

%

%

%

However, there is no information about which one of the wheels slips, longitudinal or lateral velocity, steering angle, braking force, etc.

Something caught on the wheel & objects getting into one of the wheels suddenly locking it & \cite{Alg22, Ohl19} \\
Striking pothole & when the wheel strikes a pothole, the contact point changes, diminishing the available grip, or causing an excessive deceleration & \cite{Bec19} \\
Stunt & refers to doing stunts where the standard riding configuration changes, such as one of the wheels lifts from the ground, or riding without one or both hands & \cite{Gil21, Sch12} \\
Tram track interaction & the two mechanisms discussed for tram track interaction are one of the wheels getting locked, and losing grip due to the lower available friction of track surfaces & \cite{Bec19, Her18, Ohl19, Rei25} \\
Turning mistake & no further description on the literature & \cite{Gil21, Rei25} \\
Uneven road & uneven road surfaces such as cobblestones or improperly maintained roads, cause the wheels to loose contact with the ground & \cite{Alg22, Gil21, Sch12} \\
\end{longtable}
\end{landscape}

\subsubsection{Bicycle dynamics} \label{sec: bike dynamics}
%
\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{Images/bike-dof-v3.png}
    \caption{Rotational degrees of freedom of the bicycle}
    \label{fig: dof}
\end{figure}

Bicycles are human-powered two-wheeled vehicles that require lateral balance control to maintain an upright position, which dynamics have been a matter of scientific interest for more than 100 years~\cite{Car99, Mei07, Whi99}.
%
Thus, bicycle dynamics is the study of bicycle motion and stability governed by non-linear, coupled equations involving steering, tyre forces, and rider interaction.
%
The now-classical approach for its study considers the bicycle as a dynamical system composed of four rigid bodies: rear frame, front frame, and two wheels.
%
Assuming a rider rigidly attached to the bicycle, flat ground, non-slipping tyres and permanent contact with the ground,~\cite{Mei07} presents the linearised equations of motion for the seven-degree-of-freedom model.
%%


From the linear equations of motion, the stability of the bicycle is studied, showing a self-stable behaviour and three modes of vibration: weave, capsize, and caster.
%
Weave is a low-frequency oscillatory motion involving coupled roll and steer; capsize is a non-oscillatory slow decay in roll, and caster is the self-aligning motion of the front wheel.
%
Furthermore, if components compliance is included, wobble mode arises~\cite{Sha71, Sch13}, which is an oscillatory motion of the steering, also dangerous when unstable~\cite{Lim06}.
%
From these four modes, weave and wobble are particularly interesting for their danger of loss of control when unstable.
%%


Regarding the foundations of bicycles' self-stability,~\cite{Koo11} demonstrated how it arises from several physics features of the bicycle.
%
Out of its self-stability speed range, bicycles require control strategies to maintain its stability.
%
Alternatively, bicycles ridden by humans always rely on human control to maintain upright stable balance~\cite{Koo13}.
%
To this end, the rider turns the steering into the falling side, which moves the contact point of the front wheel, shifting the equilibrium of the system.
%
Although most control actions come from the steering, at low speeds, the lower body starts to play a role in the control task~\cite{Koo09}.
%
However, the steering is not the only control input, the rider makes use of the pedals and brakes to control longitudinal acceleration and deceleration, respectively.
%%


For the human, the task of riding a bicycle consists in first maintaining balance and then, following a desired trajectory.
%
In a crash scenario, it is understood that the failure occurs in the task of maintaining balance, due to the limits of control of the rider.
%
Hence, it is useful to understand how the human controls the bicycle in a given task.
%
For this goal, \cite{Hes12} considers a neuromuscular model for the rider's arms, with bandwidth limitations and gain selection that results in a controller with similar limits in control authority as a real rider.
%
In Hess' model, the rider receives feedback from the proprioceptive, vestibular and visual systems.
%
The proprioceptive loop refers to the information received from the body, such as the position of the limbs, and the vestibular system provides information about the orientation of the head and body in space.
%%


Being that steering is the main control input and the fastest loop, through proprioceptive feedback, on the model of~\cite{Hes12}, its role is crucial for riding a bicycle.
%
Therefore, if the steering gets locked, the main balance control input disappears, losing the controllability of the system~\cite{Koo11}.
%
Consequently, disturbances to the steering negatively affect the rider's ability to control the bicycle.
%%


In addition, bicycles are sensitive to aerodynamic forces, which are studied as a resultant force acting on the centre of pressure.
%
Aerodynamic forces acting opposite to bicycle motion are known as aerodynamic drag, and its study becomes interesting for enhancing cycling performance~\cite{Deb11}.
%
Alternatively, forces acting on the lateral plane, such as cross winds, affect both steering and full-bicycle dynamics.
%
If it mainly affects the steering, it generates large steering torque depending on front wheel aerodynamics~\cite{Mao25}. 
%
If it affects the full-bicycle, the system will be pushed out of its desired trajectory.
%
For this reason, it is important to take wind into account as a possible disturbance on the system in certain conditions.
%%


Concerning bicycles under longitudinal acceleration, their limitations rely on load transfer and tyre grip~\cite{Lot21}.
%
Load transfer is the temporary redistribution of a vehicle's weight on its tyres, caused by inertial forces acting on the centre of mass.
%
On commuter riding conditions, the acceleration limit is far from being reached, different to the deceleration limit, which is subject to several factors.
%
For most bicycles, this limit is around $ 0.5 g$~\cite{Bec04, Fam20}, where $g$ is the gravitational acceleration.
%
Deceleration limit can be reached through brake actuation or collisions on the front wheel, where both cases result in pitch-over crashes~\cite{Bre10}.
%
Specific examples of reaching longitudinal load transfer limits are the so called \textit{wheelie} and \textit{stoppie}, which represent rear and forward rotations respectively in colloquial language.
%
Alternatively, if tyre friction limits are exceeded, the tyre begins to slide, causing the control forces, e.g. via steering, cannot be applied as expected.
%%


Furthermore, tyre friction also plays a major role on cornering dynamics and the rider-bicycle control loop.
%
Single-track vehicles, lean into the corner to maintain the equilibrium of forces, see Figure~\ref{fig: cornering}.
%
These manoeuvres generate large lateral forces on the tyres, which are supported by friction between the rubber and the ground.
%
Hence, friction on the tyre-ground interface is a complex phenomenon of at least three mechanisms: adhesion, deformation and wear~\cite{Han03}.
%
Adhesion refers to the bonding between both surfaces, while deformation is the capability of the tyre to adapt to the road irregularities.
%
Along, wear occurs when local stress on the tyre surpasses its elastic limit, causing tearing and dissipating energy, which results in friction.
%
Therefore, when there is an external agent on this interface, such as water, dust or another layer of material, tyre friction relies only on deformation~\cite{Mic01}.
%
The absence or reduction of longitudinal and lateral forces acting on the tyres, limit the control of the system through the steering and produces unpredictable behaviour.
%%


Tyre friction is a non-linear behaviour on lateral and longitudinal loads, which has a saturation limit at a given force.
%
In an idealised case where tyres have maximum adherence and no side-slip, the vehicle follows a perfect trajectory as shown in Figure~\ref{fig: cornering-topview}.
%
However, within the real operating range of a tyre, its slip angle follows the definition in Section~\ref{subsec: terminology terminology}.
%
Depending on the side-slip angle of the front and rear axles, the vehicle can present an understeering, neutral, or oversteering behaviour~\cite{Cos06}.
%
Understeering occurs when front side-slip is larger than rear, therefore, the vehicle turns less than expected, see Figure~\ref{fig: understeering-case}.
%
Contrarily, oversteering occurs when rear side-slip is larger than the front, thus, the vehicle turns more than the steering input, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig: oversteering-case}.
%
When both slips are balanced, the vehicle behaves neutral (cf. Figure~\ref{fig: neutral-steering-case}), following the path determined by the user through the steering.
%
Thus, the cornering behaviour of the bicycle will influence the crash configuration when the tyre-ground interface mechanism fails.
%%


Consequently, when the saturation limit is reached, the tyre starts to exhibit a phenomenon called `skidding'. 
%
The extent of loss of grip will lead to different crash configurations (see Section~\ref{subsec: crash motion}), which is particularly interesting to understand single-cyclist crashes.
%
Therefore, cornering and tyre dynamics are fundamental to understand bicycle crashes.
%

The human control loop is studied as dependent on the feedback from the two main rotational degrees of freedom of the system, steer and roll.

Within the motion of the system, we find longitudinal and lateral dynamics that face different challenges in their interaction with the environment.
%
Thus, the bicycle-rider system is a complex dynamical system that requires the above understanding of its dynamics to assess its failure mechanisms.

\subsection{Sequential crash classification}
%
Our classification follows a sequential structure, assessing crashes as a series of discrete events in a specific time, also known in the literature as `Linear chain-of-failure event model'~\cite{Lev20}.
%
The crash classification consists of five main classes composed of already known concepts in the field, such as the manoeuvre executed prior to the crash, the nature of the perturbation on the system, the mechanisms of the crash, the level of recoverability, and the motion of the system during the crash.
%
% We further extend these classes by introducing intermediate classes that address specific gaps: human control in crash or pre-crash scenarios, characteristics of the excitation of the system, and the spatial location of the elements of the system.
% %
% The spatial location is the information on whether the mechanisms act on the front or rear wheel, or if the perturbation, if it exists, acts longitudinally, laterally, or vertically on the system.
% %
% Our intention is to bring the attention to the characteristics of the perturbation signal, the rider control on critical scenarios, and the spatial location where mechanisms act on the system, which complement the information of the main classes of our classification system described herein.
% 


\subsubsection{Pre-crash manoeuvre}
%
In our classification framework, the first step consists of identifying the manoeuvre executed by the rider in the moment prior to the crash, which we categorise as follows:
%
\begin{description}
    \item [Longitudinal acceleration:] braking and acceleration, both purposely performed by the user.
    
    \item [Low speed riding:] start and stop riding, where users require major balance actions to maintain the bicycle in an upright position. 
    %
    Speed ranges between 0.8 and 2.8 [m/s], which is below the average user speed \cite{Cla19}.
    
    \item [Steady and planned cornering:] the rider is performing a controlled turn, with a clear trajectory in mind.
    
    \item [Straight-heading:] the rider is following a straight path, maintaining balance through minor steering adjustments and no major variations on the states of the system.
    
    \item [Stunting:] any action that is not intended for riding, such as riding with one hand, no hands, riding on one wheel, or purposely transitioning to a challenging-to-control state, which changes and limits the control of the rider on the bicycle.
    
    \item [Unplanned turning or avoidance manoeuvre:] the rider executes a sudden evasive turn to avoid an obstacle.
\end{description}



\subsubsection{Nature of the perturbation}
%
Due to the complexity of the human-bicycle system, it is subjected to several perturbations, which we approach in a way aligned with \cite{Fel76} and \cite{Sch12}, who refer to them as direct causes of the crash.
%
How the interplay between these perturbations occur is shown in Figure \ref{fig: rider-bike diagram}. 
%
Here, we refer to them as rider, bicycle, and external, respectively, which description follows:
%
\begin{description}
    \item [Bicycle:] any perturbation that occurs due to the behaviour of the bicycle itself. 
    %
    This encompasses the stability of the bicycle and mechanical malfunctions.

    \item [Rider:] the perturbations on the system that come from the control actions of the rider to the bicycle.
    
    \item [External:] any perturbation, force, change of state, or influence exerted by the environment on the system.
\end{description}

\subsubsection{Mechanisms of crash}
%

% Make each mechanism an independent piece and do more back references to connect them all together rather than explaining twice the same.

In the literature, there are several known crash descriptors, normally clustered as skidding, collisions, falls, among others, as seen in Table \ref{tab: known descriptors}.
%
However, these concepts do not fit the broad spectrum of mechanisms, nor capture the dynamics involved in the events.
%
For this reason, we collect them in physically-coherent categories, described as:
%
\begin{description}
    \item [Balance control:] the ability of the human to balance the bicycle as an inverted pendulum, moving the steering to control its vertical equilibrium.
    %%
    \item [Collision forces:] any collision or external force exerted on the bicycle, excluding tyre-ground friction interactions.
    %%
    \item [Load transfer:] as explained in Section \ref{sec: bike dynamics}, this is the temporary redistribution of the weight on the wheels caused by longitudinal acceleration or deceleration.
    %
    When this phenomenon is excessive, the system initiates a rotational motion around one of the wheels, which usually goes beyond the boundaries of control of the rider, causing a crash.
    %%
    \item [Rider-bicycle interface:] the five interfaces between the rider and the bicycle: handlebar (both hands), pedals, and saddle. 
    %
    The loss of one or more of these interfaces reduces the control authority, therefore, increasing the task demand while reducing rider capability.
    %%
    \item [Tyre friction:] the tyre-ground contact interface, which is the only solid connection between the bicycle and the environment.
    %
    As detailed in Section \ref{sec: bike dynamics}, tyres have a saturation limit for the grip available.
    %
    Reaching the limit of available grip implies that tyres start to skid and the rider loses control of the vehicle. 
    %
    The loss of control occurs due to an imbalance of the lateral forces shown in Figure \ref{fig: cornering}.
    %
    Depending on which of the tyres loses grip, the dynamics will change, causing under- or over-steering behaviour.
    %%
\end{description}

\subsubsection{Characteristic motion} \label{subsec: crash motion}
%
The last stage of a crash, the connection between the crash and post-crash phases, which also causes more visual impact, is the motion of the system as a result of the crash mechanism.
%
The three characteristic motions for two-wheeled single-track vehicle crashes that are commonly discussed in the literature \cite{Bed16, Ros17}, are schematically represented in Figures \ref{fig: highside}, \ref{fig: lowside}, and \ref{fig: pitchover}. 
%
Note that motorcycle crash analyses are referenced to explain these crash motions, since the available bicycle-related literature is scarce and does not provide a detailed description.
%%


From Section \ref{sec: bike dynamics}, we know that crash motions occur directly related to two of the angular degrees of freedom of the rear frame of the bicycle, i.e. pitch and roll.
%
However, due to the unstable nature of bicycle lateral dynamics, roll-related crash motions are sub-divided in three different cases.
%
Consequently, we describe the crash motions as:
%
\begin{description}
    \item [Collision ejection:] crashes where the final motion is not clear, the main event is a deceleration that stops the motion of the bicycle, while the rider gets ejected.
    %%

    \item [Pitch-over:] one of the wheels lifts from the ground, following a trajectory that finishes with the front wheel behind the rear wheel, see Figure \ref{fig: pitchover} \cite{Bec04, Bre10}.
    %
    Colloquially known as uncontrolled \textit{stoppie} or \textit{wheelie}, depending on whether the motion goes towards the front or the rear, respectively.
    %%

    \item [Low-side:] the rider-bicycle system follows an excessive roll rate in the same direction as at the beginning, see Figure \ref{fig: lowside}. 
    %
    \cite{Cos07} explain it as a yaw movement that causes over-steering rotation.
    %
    The underlying mechanism is the tyre-friction, since the available force from the ground reduces, the system loses its balance and the fall occurs.
    %%

    \item [High-side:] a skidding motion followed by sudden deceleration of the wheel while in lateral motion, which leads to a high negative roll rate with respect to the direction of the initial motion, see Figure \ref{fig: highside}.
    %
    \cite{Cos07} add the characteristic of rider upward ejection as a result, which frequently occurred in racing motorcycle crashes.
    %%

    \item [Tip-over:] the bicycle falls into one side as a result of a lack of control at low or no speed. 
    %
    Differs from the low-side crash by the absence of skidding since the underlying mechanism is tied to the balance control from the rider rather than the tyre dynamics.
    %
    Moreover, this is one of the easily detected cases through inertial measurement sensing as shown in \cite{Sch21}.
    %%

\end{description}
%

\subsection{Questionnaire results}
%
Each respondent of the questionnaire received a batch of ten videos, then, we used a score system to interpret the results.
%
Each batch had a weight based on the number of respondents, calculated as $weight = \frac{n^{\circ} \textrm{responses batch}}{\textrm{total responses}}$.
%
For pre-crash manoeuvre, nature of the perturbation, and motion of the crash, we divided the total of matching responses by the total respondents in the batch.
%
For crash mechanisms, we divided the number of mechanisms on agreement by multiple respondents by the total of options that respondents indicated as having a role.
%
Concerning the disagreement, we considered complete disagreement only if there was no option chosen by two or more respondents.
%
At last, the final score was calculated as
%
\begin{equation}
    score = \frac{\textrm{agreement responses}}{\textrm{total responses}} \cdot weight.
\end{equation}
%%


Our questionnaire was answered by 17 participants, and the results are shown in Table \ref{tab: questionnaire}.
%
The main result is the agreement above 75\% for pre-crash manoeuvre, nature of the perturbation (NoP), and crash motion.
%
Conversely, the overall agreement concerning mechanisms is rather low, with only 54\%.
%
However, only 1\% of the videos showed disagreement in all the mechanisms.
%
The interpretation of these results is exposed on the discussion.
%
