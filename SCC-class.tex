\subsection{Key concepts}

Here, we provide a short definition of the key concepts that will be used during the conceptualization of the boundaries of rider-bicycle behaviour.
%%


First, during this article, we will refer to the bicycle and the human coupled together in motion as a dynamical system, which is modelled by non-linear differential equations.
%
Assuming the rider as a controller of the system, we can identify two characteristics: \textit{controllability} and \textit{rideability}.
%
The former means that all system variables can be influenced by the input \citep{Sef01, Ver12}, while the latter will be assummed as the capacity of using the vehicle in its design form.
%%

Second, a perturbation will be understood as any external force applied to the rider or to the bicycle, which changes the state of the system.
%
An active perturbation is a controlled force from an external source, such as other vehicle colliding with the system.
%
A passive perturbation is a non-controlled force, e.g. the force excerted by road irregularities on bicycle tyres.
%%

Third, we will call a crash to any sudden and unintended event that results in a disruption of the normal and controlled motion of the human-bicycle system.
%%

% Jason: Add diagram to explain crash phases. > Contrast Park definition with this new defintion for bicycles

Fourth, in the analysis of vehicular collisions, there are three phases: pre-impact motion, impact motion and post-impact motion \citep{Par01}.
%
Translating this approach to bicycle accidents, pre-crash motion encompasses the time window from the perturbation to the moment where the bicycle is no longer rideable.
%
Then, the crash stage begins, until the moment when the system stops its motion.
%
Post-crash stage results limited in SBCs, being mostly a time frame for rider movements after the bicycle stopped.
%%


Finally, bicycles and motorcycles share characteristics as a dynamical systems, which makes them similar for studies.
%
In its analytical study, both are usually assumed as a four-bodies single-track vehicle \cite{Sha71, Mei07, Lot21}, with two basic degrees of freedom: roll $\varphi$ and steering $\delta$.

Both vehicles experience similar crash configurations \citep{Haw13, Ken20}, including tyre skidding, inability of control the system through the steering, excessive pitch rotation, among others later discussed in this paper.

\subsection{Classification of bicycle crashes} % Move this to methodology/exclusion criteria and summarise.

A detailed description of the types of bicycle crashes and their conditions can be found in \citep{Dep22}.
%
However, in the sake of simplicity we will group them in four categories like \cite{Gil24}, focused on the interaction of the human-bicycle system with the environment:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Single Bicycle Crash (SBC):} it is when the cyclist loses the ability to control the bicycle due to falls or impacts not involving another road user.
    \item \textbf{Bicycle-Bicycle Crash (BBC):} the crash occurs between two or more bicycles.
    \item \textbf{Motor-Vehicle Bicycle Crash (MBC):} this category includes collisions between bicycles and motor vehicles such as cars, trucks or motorcycles. 
    \item \textbf{Pedestrian Bicycle Crash (PBC):} interactions where the cyclist collides with a pedestrian and the outcome for the cyclist is a fall.
\end{enumerate}


Since the aim of the project is to find and understand the mechanisms of the human-bicycle system that cause the crash, we will focus only in SBCs.

%
In other words, the time boundary of human response is at 200 [\unit{\m\s}], which will be called the Response Threshold (RT).
%
If the combination of states that are outside of rideability of the bicycle occurs before of 200 [\unit{\m\s}] since the start of the motion, the system is outside of its control boundaries.
%%

Britten analyses the changes in the dynamics of pitch-over accidents for different causes, comparing excessive front braking, front wheel lock by an object, and frontal crash.
%
Surprisingly, one of the findings was that in these kind of accidents, the front wheel lifts off before the Centre of Mass (CoM) reaches the vertical position with respect to its contact point.
%
The two possible reasons hypothesised are: potential energy stored in the elements of the system during the deceleration phase and inertia of the bodies in the rotation phase.
%
For the objective of the present article, we can highlight that, in this case, the PNR is more related to kinetic energy than to a state of the system such as pitch angle.
%
In other words, the PNR shows to be the moment where the front wheel loses contact with the ground.



Additionally, from \cite{Lot21}, we know that the maximum possible deceleration to avoid pitch-over motion is given by the equation
%
\begin{equation}
    |a_x| < \frac{(w-b)g}{h},
\end{equation}
%
where $h$ and $b$ are the vertical and longitudinal CoM position, while $w$ is the wheelbase and $g$ is the gravitational acceleration.
%
Using the parameters from \cite{Mei07}, the maximum deceleration is 7.722 [\unit{\m\per\s\squared}] = 0.787$g$, similar to the 0.7$g$ found by \cite{Bre10}.
%
Therefore, we can define a boundary of bicycle longitudinal deceleration in 6,87 [\unit{\m\per\s\squared}].
%


A braking manoeuvre over 0.7$g$ will trigger a critical situation where rear wheel lifts from the ground, thus, the time to counteract the effect will be limited to the moment when the CoM is vertically aligned with the front wheel contact point.



With analogous analysis, we could explore the maximum roll rate that the rider is capable to handle and the maximum slipping related to the roll rate.
%
For slipping and starting accidents.
%
In the case of external perturbations, it will be necessary to analyse the maximum steering angle that the rider can handle.
