\section{Introduction}


Cycling population is on arise due to the advantages of the bicycle as a commuter vehicle.
%
This refers from almost free transportation and less polution to healthier lifestiles and less traffic congestion.
%
However, as the cycling population increases, their exposure to crashes also increase.
%%

Regarding bicycle crashes, one configuration that stands out due to their major share on statistics are the Single-Cyclist Crashes (SCC) \citep{Weg24}.
%
These are events where no other road user is involved as an active perturbation, i.e. its effect does not depend on the source.
%
Its share ranges from 50\% to 85\% of cyclist-related hospital admissions \cite{}.
%
Moreover, it is known that these events are commonly underreported \cite{Boe17, Bec19, Sch21, Utr20, Utr23}.
%%


In the last decade, Europe has seen a reduction in the fatality rate of total road accidents, except for bicycle crashes \citep{CBS24, Com24, Gil24, Sal22, Utr23}.
%
Indeed, in the Netherlands the risk of death in SCCs has not only remained constant, but increased up to 7\% \citep{Sch17}. 
%
Therefore, it is a matter of interest when bicycle usage is intended to be promoted and risks need to be minimised.
%
Furthermore, if these issues are not attended, bicycle usage can be expected to decrease \citep{Gu19}
%
However, the dynamics of these crashes are not well understood \cite{Haw13}. % Revise cite.
%
Therefore, research on the dynamics of SCCs is needed to continue developing cyclists road safety.
%%



Up to this point, most work has been done in descriptive analysis of common factors that lead to SCCs \citep{Bec19, Gil24, Her18, Sal22, Sch11, Sch12, Sch15, Utr23}.
%
Within these descriptive analyses we find common factors and mechanism with a major role in the crash.
%
However, none of these classifications take into account the particular dynamics of the bicycle.
%
Therefore, the aim of this article is to review current classifications and develop a bicycle dynamics based classification of SCCs.


The importance of provide a classification based on bicycle dynamics is to highlight the challenges that are needed to overcome to continue developing the safety of the bicycles.
Also to better understand what are the key factors that casuse bicycle crashes and propose more effective countermeasures.

\subsection{Key concepts}

Since some concepts in this matter are not standarised, we include important definitions used in this work.
%

First, the events of interest receive different names: single-bicycle crash, single-actor bicycle crash, single-cyclist crash, among others (see Table \ref{tab: results}).
%
On one hand, the expresion single-bicycle crash neglects the rider, making reference to a problem only oriented on the bicycle.
%
On the other hand, single-actor bicycle crashes assumes that there are no other actors involved in the event, which neglects indirect effects of other road users, e.g. pedestrian entering into the bike lane and triggering an avoidance manoeuvre that ends in a crash.
%
In addition, any other denomination that includes the word `accident' is not appropiate due to the preventable nature of these events \citep{Dav01}.
%
Therefore, for this work we adopt single-cyclist crash, due to its  reference to the rider and the bicycle as the main actors in the event, allowing other indirect actors.
%%


Second, during this article, we will refer to the bicycle and the human coupled together in motion as a dynamical system, which is modelled by non-linear differential equations.
%
Assuming the rider as a controller of the system, we identify the existence of \textit{controllability}, which means that all system variables can be influenced by the input \citep{Sef01, Ver12}.
%%


Third, a perturbation will be understood by its dictionary definition as any variation in the system that causes it to change its regular movement \citep{Cam25}.
%
An active perturbation is a controlled force from an external source, such as other vehicle colliding with the system.
%
A passive perturbation is a non-controlled force, e.g. the force excerted by road irregularities on bicycle tyres.
%%


% Jason: Add diagram to explain crash phases. > Contrast Park definition with this new defintion for bicycles
Fourth, we will call a crash to any sudden and unintended event that results in a disruption of the normal and controlled motion of the human-bicycle system.
%
In the analysis of vehicular collisions, there are three phases: pre-impact motion, impact motion and post-impact motion \citep{Par01}.
%
Translating this approach to bicycle crashes, pre-crash motion encompasses the time window from the perturbation to the moment where the bicycle is no longer controllable.
%
Then, the crash stage begins, until the moment when the system stops its motion.
%
Post-crash stage is limited in SCCs and can be assessed as the moment when the vehicle and the rider are disconnected and presenting independent motions \citep{Llo16}.
%%


At last, bicycles and motorcycles share characteristics as a dynamical systems, which makes them similar for studies.
%
In its analytical study, both are usually assumed as a four-bodies single-track vehicle \cite{Sha71, Mei07, Lot21}, with two basic degrees of freedom: roll $\varphi$ and steering $\delta$.
%
Both vehicles experience similar crash configurations \citep{Haw13, Ken20, Ll016}, including tyre sliding, inability of control the system through the steering, excessive pitch rotation, among others later discussed in this paper.
%
The main differences are the mass, the working speed range, and power input from the user, being this last what defines if the vehicle is a bicycle or a motorcycle.
%
Despite some modern electric bicycles include advanced suspension systems, heavy batteries and electric motors that increase overall mass, and are capable of reaching speeds up to 45 [km/h], they are still dependant on rider force for longitudinal motion.


\subsection{Research target}

The questions that shape the present article are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item What are the common approaches to study and analyse human-bicycle dynamics and control in crash scenarions?
    \item Is there a classification of crashes that captures the particular behaviour of the human-bicycle system?
    \item Is there single-cyclist crash data that is useful to study the dynamics of these events?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Inclusion and exclusion criteria}

The inclusion criteria for the snowballing technique was:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The study presented statistics related to single-cyclist crashes.
    \item The study takes into account the dynamics of the bicycle for the crash analysis.
    \item Studies concerning human control of vehicles.
    \item Studies that explain the techniques to control a bicycle.
    \item Analyses on bicycle dynamics that explain its rideability and controllability.
\end{itemize}

On the other hand, the exclusion of articles was based on the following criteria: 

\begin{itemize}
    \item Studies focused only on injury outcomes.
    \item Analyses of crashes of bicycles with other road users.
    \item Articles in a language different than English.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Antilock Braking System (ABS)}

This system consists in a constant reading of vehicle's wheels speed and it is capable of detecting when a wheel locks, decreasing the braking pressure and maintain the wheel spining while diminishing the velocity.
%
The main result in four-wheeled vehicles have been the possibility of maintain the control of the vehicle during the whole braking manoeuvre.

%
Similarly, \citep{Teo22} found a 22\% reduction of fatal crashes per year with ABS motorcycles.


\subsubsection{Stability Control (SC)}

%
This system have been implemented in high-end automobiles for more than 20 years, however, nowadays is a more common feature among mid-range cars.
%
The reduction of fatal rollover crashes is estimated about 70 - 90\% \citep{Fer07}
%
Moreover, its impact has shown to reduce risk of single-vehicle accidents by 31\% \citep{Lyc15}.

\section{The Human} \label{sec: human}

In the aim of characterise the PNR, we look for Rider Physiological Limits (RPLs), which are defined by \cite{Sch13} as:
%
`\textit{...human limitations such as the time required to sense a change in a state (time delay), the maximum force a rider can apply, the maximum speed a limb can move and its maximum acceleration.}'


To know what are the RPLs, it is necessary to delve into human biomechanics of already analysed situations such as fall or slip in gait.



\cite{The96} performed experiments to measure the time required to develop 15 [\unit{\N\m}] of torque in ankles, comparing between males and females, young and old adults.
%
His experiments used a visual cue to trigger the response, which had a delay of 157 [\unit{\m\s}] for young males (YM) and 141 [\unit{\m\s}] for young females (YF).
%
The time to develop 15 [Nm] was of 74 and 95 [ms] for YM and YF, respectively.
%%


Likewise, in forward fall accidents, recovery step has been proved to occur at an average constant time of 293 [ms] \citep{Do99}.
%
However, the first reaction detected takes place around 85.4 and 100.2 [ms], which corresponds to reflex response to the fall \citep{Do82}.



A different approach to forward fall accidents can be found in \citep{Van02}, where the threshold for a fall is given by tilt angle.
%
The working model was based in a rigid inverted pendulum, which suppose a limitation to results due to its simplicity compared to real biomechanics.
%
After all, his work concludes that the maximum angles where a recovery is possible are between 23\textdegree and 26\textdegree.
%
Additionally, it was found an increase of 77\% in safe walking by reducing response time from 267 to 175 [ms]. 



The boundaries of slipping accidents have been studied by \cite{Str81}, where it was found that slip motion starts on average 0.05 [\unit{\s}] after heel contact in low-grip surfaces.
%
The maximum slip distance where the result was not a fall was 0.1 [m], along with a slip velocity of 0.5 [\unit{\m\per\s}].
%
With these conclusions, Strandberg indicates that the friction properties of shoe-floor interface are most important for speeds below 0.5 [\unit{\m\per\s}].
%%

\section{Characterization of the point of no return} \label{sec: PNR}

As stated in the previous section, the average response time for different accidents is around 200 - 300 [\unit{\m\s}], which gives us the first characteristic for the PNR: the time window.
%
In other words, the time boundary of human response is at 200 [\unit{\m\s}], which will be called the Response Threshold (RT).
%
If the combination of states that are outside of rideability of the bicycle occurs before of 200 [\unit{\m\s}] since the start of the motion, the system is outside of its control boundaries.
%%


Then, taking into account the findings from \cite{Str81} and \cite{Cha02a}, both results are consistent with the time window.
%
Considering the 200 [\unit{\m\s}] time, at 0.5 [\unit{\m\per\s}], the slipped distance is 0.1 [m], which is in accordance with conclusions of \cite{Str81}.
%
Similarly, in same time, at 0.8 [\unit{\m\s}], the slipped distance results in 0.16 [m], which would result in a fall according to \cite{Cha02a}.
%
Therefore, the most basic characterisation of PNR for human falls from slipping is 0.14 [m] and 0.8 [\unit{\m\per\s}] within 200 [\unit{\m\s}] since slipping starts.
%
When any of these states is reached before the RT, the system surpases the PNR, since any response will continue outside of the boundaries for human standing.
%%


Regarding forward falls, the maximum angle is 26\textdegree \citep{Van02}, while applying the RT, the maximum angular velocity results in 2.27 [\unit{\radian\per\s}].
%%


Concerning bicycle crashes, we could not find literature where crash beahaviour is mathematically described, the closest approach is \cite{Bre10}, where pitch-over crashes are analysed.
%
Britten analyses the changes in the dynamics of pitch-over accidents for different causes, comparing excessive front braking, front wheel lock by an object, and frontal crash.
%
Surprisingly, one of the findings was that in these kind of accidents, the front wheel lifts off before the Centre of Mass (CoM) reaches the vertical position with respect to its contact point.
%
The two possible reasons hypothesised are: potential energy stored in the elements of the system during the deceleration phase and inertia of the bodies in the rotation phase.
%
For the objective of the present article, we can highlight that, in this case, the PNR is more related to kinetic energy than to a state of the system such as pitch angle.
%
In other words, the PNR shows to be the moment where the front wheel loses contact with the ground.



Additionally, from \cite{Lot21}, we know that the maximum possible deceleration to avoid pitch-over motion is given by the equation
%
\begin{equation}
    |a_x| < \frac{(w-b)g}{h},
\end{equation}
%
where $h$ and $b$ are the vertical and longitudinal CoM position, while $w$ is the wheelbase and $g$ is the gravitational acceleration.
%
Using the parameters from \cite{Mei07}, the maximum deceleration is 7.722 [\unit{\m\per\s\squared}] = 0.787$g$, similar to the 0.7$g$ found by \cite{Bre10}.
%
Therefore, we can define a boundary of bicycle longitudinal deceleration in 6,87 [\unit{\m\per\s\squared}].
%


A braking manoeuvre over 0.7$g$ will trigger a critical situation where rear wheel lifts from the ground, thus, the time to counteract the effect will be limited to the moment when the CoM is vertically aligned with the front wheel contact point.
