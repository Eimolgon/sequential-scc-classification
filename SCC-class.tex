\section{Introduction} \label{sec: introduction}


Cycling population is on arise due to the advantages of the bicycle as a commuter vehicle.
%
This refers from almost free transportation and less polution to healthier lifestyles and less traffic congestion.
%
However, as the cycling population increases, their exposure to crashes also increase \citep{Com24, Utr23}. 
%%


Regarding bicycle crashes, one configuration that stands out due to their major share on statistics are the Single-Cyclist Crashes (SCC) \citep{Weg24}.
%
These are events where no other road user is involved as an active perturbation, i.e. its effect does not depend on the source.
%
Its share ranges from 50\% to 85\% of cyclist-related hospital admissions \cite{Alg22, Bec19}.
%
Moreover, it is known that these events are commonly underreported \cite{Boe17, Bec19, Sch21, Utr20, Utr23}.
%%


In the last decade, Europe has seen a reduction in the fatality rate of total road accidents, except for bicycle crashes \citep{CBS24, Com24, Gil24, Sal22, Utr23}.
%
Indeed, in the Netherlands the risk of death in SCCs has not only remained constant, but increased up to 7\% \citep{Sch17}. 
%
Hence, it is a matter of interest when bicycle usage is intended to be promoted and risks need to be minimised.
%
Furthermore, if these issues are not attended, bicycle usage can be expected to decrease \citep{Gu19}
%
However, the dynamics of these crashes are not well understood and more detailed crash information is needed \citep{Haw13, Mol19}. % Revise cite Haw.
%
Therefore, research on the dynamics of SCCs is needed to continue developing cyclists road safety.
%%


A key factor on the increasing share of bicycle crashes is the constant reduction of motorised vehicle crashes \citep{Swo25}.
%
These vehicles have been benefited by several driving asssitance systems, such as stability control, anti-lock braking systems, and collision avoidance systems, among others \citep{Ben14}.
%
Hence, the load for the driver has been reduced, giving them a wider window to make mistakes without leading to a crash.
%
Contrarily, bicycles have remained almost unchanged throughout the years, with the exception of electric pedal assistance, which facilitates its use among older users and consequently increases the risk of crashes \citep{Kap22}.
%
Therefore, seems natural to think about increasing the safety of bicycles as vehicles on its own, along with other road safety measures.
%
To this end, it is necessary to understand the dynamics of the human-bicycle system in pre-crash and high-risk scenarios, which will guide the future development of safety systems.


% For cars, crash reconstruction have been a vital approach to understand the failure mechanisms and provide countermeasures \citep{}.

% In addition, since the early 2000, cars started to integrate a plenty of sensors, which track relevant information of the vehicle in normal and critical scenarios, allowing researchers to understand the key factors.

% Since accident recornstruction is extremely difficult for bicycles and motorcycles due to the lack of traces in the environment, different approaches are needed.



Up to this point, most work has been done in descriptive analysis of common factors that lead to SCCs \citep{Bec19, Gil24, Her18, Sal22, Sch11, Sch12, Sch15, Utr23}.
%
Within these descriptive analyses we find common factors and hypothesised mechanisms with a major role in the crash.
%
However, none of these classifications take into account the particular dynamics of the bicycle. %, neglecting the gap in the lack of vital information to completely understand the dynamics of the crashes.
%
Therefore, the aim of this article is to review current classifications and develop a bicycle dynamics-based classification of single-cylcist crashes.
%
With this, we highlight the research gap on the lack of information required to completely understand the dynamics of these events.


\subsection{Research target}

The questions that shape the present article are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item What are the common approaches to study and analyse human-bicycle dynamics and control in crash scenarios?
    \item Is there a classification of crashes that captures the particular behaviour of the human-bicycle system?
    \item Is there single-cyclist crash data that is useful to study the dynamics of these events?
\end{enumerate}



The article is structured as follows, Section \ref{sec: methods} describes the search query, inclusion and exclusion criteria and the classification methodology.
%
Section \ref{sec: results} presents the key concepts used in this work, along with the main results from the literature and an overview of bicycle dynamics theory.
%
The proposed classification is developed and explained on Section \ref{sec: newclassification}.
%
Finally, Sections \ref{sec: discussion} and \ref{sec: conclusion} present the discussion and conclusion, respectively.



\section{Methods} \label{sec: methods}


The literature search was made in Google Scholar and Dimensions database in October 2024.
%
The interest relied on studies that analysed the dynamics of single-track vehicle crashes, which include bicycles, motorcycles and scooters.
%
Therefore, the initial query for the broad range search on October 2024 was:

\begin{center}
    (bicycle OR bike OR two-wheel* OR single-track OR ``two wheel*'' OR ``single track'') AND (crash OR collision OR accident OR fall).
\end{center}

The results of the search provided the main articles in the field, however, we used `snowballing' methodology to complement the search.
%
This approach was particularly useful to cover base concepts that are not always clarified.
%
In addition, this methodology lead us to articles with no direct relation, such as vehicular crash analysis and requirements.


\subsection{Inclusion and exclusion criteria}

The inclusion criteria for the literature was:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The study presented statistics related to single-cyclist crashes.
    \item The study takes into account the dynamics of the bicycle for the crash analysis.
    \item Studies concerning human control of vehicles.
    \item Studies that explain the techniques to control a bicycle.
    \item Analyses on bicycle dynamics that explain its rideability and controllability.
\end{itemize}

On the other hand, the exclusion of articles was based on the following criteria: 

\begin{itemize}
    \item Studies focused only on injury outcomes.
    \item Analyses of crashes of bicycles with other road users.
    \item Articles in a language different than English.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Key concepts} \label{subsec: keyconcepts}

Since some concepts in this matter are not standardised, we include important definitions used in this work.
%

First, the events of interest receive different names: single-bicycle crash, single-actor bicycle crash \citep{Ali23}, single-cyclist crash, among others (see Table \ref{tab: results}).
%
On one hand, the expression single-bicycle crash, even being the most common (see Table \ref{tab: results}), neglects the rider, making reference to a problem only oriented on the bicycle.
%
On the other hand, single-actor bicycle crashes assumes that there are no other actors involved in the event, which neglects indirect effects of other road users, e.g. a pedestrian entering into the bike lane triggering an avoidance manoeuvre that ends in a crash.
%
In addition, any other denomination that includes the word `accident' is not appropriate due to the preventable nature of these events \citep{Dav01}.
%
Therefore, for this work we adopt single-cyclist crash, due to its reference to the rider and the bicycle as the main actors in the event, allowing other indirect actors.
%%


Second, during this article, we will refer to the bicycle and the human coupled together in motion as a dynamical system, which is modelled by non-linear differential equations.
%
Assuming the rider as a controller of the system, we identify the existence of \textit{controllability}, which means that all system variables can be influenced by the input \citep{Sef01, Ver12}.
%%


Third, a perturbation will be understood by its dictionary definition as any variation in the system that causes it to change its regular movement \citep{Cam25}.
%
An active perturbation is a controlled force from an external source, such as other vehicle colliding with the system.
%
A passive perturbation is a non-controlled force, e.g. the force exerted by road irregularities on bicycle tyres.
%%


% Jason: Add diagram to explain crash phases. > Contrast Park definition with this new defintion for bicycles
Fourth, we will call a crash to any sudden and unintended event that results in a disruption of the normal and controlled motion of the human-bicycle system.
%
In the analysis of vehicular collisions, there are three phases: pre-impact motion, impact motion and post-impact motion \citep{Par01}.
%
Translating this approach to bicycle crashes, pre-crash motion encompasses the time window from the perturbation to the moment where the bicycle is no longer controllable.
%
Then, the crash stage occurs, until the moment when the system stops its motion.
%
Post-crash stage is limited in SCCs and can be assessed as the moment when the vehicle and the rider are disconnected and presenting independent motions \citep{Llo16}.
%%


Fifth, although categorisation and classification are used as synonyms, both terms have different meaning based on its background.
%
The former refers to a more creative and flexible set of divisions to group events that share similarities.
%
The latter is used when the divisions are based on an objective and defined criteria, which is the case in the present work.
% 




Sixth, a common factor in bicycle crashes are events caused by failure in the adherence between the tyre and the road.
%
Tyre-ground interaction is a complex phenomenon later discussed in this work, however, we find different concepts, which althought similar, are different and can lead to confusion.
%
The term `slip' refers to the relative motion between the tyre and the ground, which can be longitudinal or lateral and constitutes one of the main characteristics of tyres \citep{Pac06}.
%
Therefore, it should not be used to refer to the excessive lateral motion of the tyres as a result of reaching its saturation limit, i.e. when the tyre can not handle more lateral load and the friction force reaches its limit.
%
Most authors refer to them as `skidding' \citep{Gil24}, which is a more appropriate concept to describe the loss of control due to the lack of grip on the tyre-ground interface.
%
Additionally, its dictionary definition refers to it as a term specific for describing out-of-control slide on vehicles \citep{Cam25}.



At last, bicycles and motorcycles share the main characteristics as dynamical systems, which makes them similar for studies \citep{Lim06}.
%
In its analytical study, both are usually assumed as four-bodies single-track vehicles \cite{Sha71, Mei07, Lot21}, with two basic degrees of freedom: roll $\varphi$ and steering $\delta$.
%
Both vehicles experience similar crash configurations \citep{Haw13, Ken20, Ll016}, including tyre skidding, inability of control the system through the steering, excessive pitch rotation, among others later discussed in this paper.
%
The main differences are the mass, the working speed range, and power input from the user, being this last what we use to define if the vehicle is a bicycle or a motorcycle.
%
Despite some modern electric bicycles include advanced suspension systems, heavy batteries and electric motors that increase overall mass, and are capable of reaching speeds up to 45 [km/h], they are still dependant on rider force for longitudinal motion.
%%


\subsection{Single-cyclist crash anatomy} \label{subsec: crash}


In the most basic understanding of a single-cyclist crash, this event is the result of the rider losing control of the vehicle.
%
\cite{Ful05} describes the in his model, the loss of control as a relation between task demand and rider capability, when the task demand surpasses rider capability, the rider loses control and the crash occurs.
%
This model considers physical characteristics and knowledge of the driver on what to do under determined circumstances to define the boundaries its capability.
%
However, this capacity is also affected by other factors related to the circumstance, such as awareness, emotion, fatigue, among others.
%
On the other hand, task demanding elements encompass travelling speed, environmental factors, such as road characteristics and other road users.
%
Of these factors, the driver has direct control of the travelling speed, achieving balance between driver capacity and its awareness to the task.
%
While car drivers can reduce task demand by decreasing speed, this is not always the case for bicycle riders, due to the increase of user stabilisation requirements at low speeds \cite{Koo11, Sch13, Vla15}.
%
In general, the theory shows an almost constant driver capability balancing with changing task demands, which should not surpass the capability threshold.




When the task demand increases exponentially, possibly leading to a loss of control, it is understood as a critical scenario.
%
Factors that increase task demand and contribute to critical scenarios are known in the literature.
%
As seen in Table \ref{tab: results}, the most repeated events are skidding, low speed riding, braking, `loss of control', collision with an object and interactions with tram tracks.

\subsection{Single-cyclist crash classifications} \label{subsec: classifications}


Currently, studies on single-cyclist crashes are based on the available data, which is mainly descriptive from written reports, see Table \ref{tab: results}.
%
Within these studies, the main one that presents a classification comes from \cite{Sch12}, where we find the three components needed for a crash: rider, bicycle and environment, analogous to the analysis for car crashes made by \cite{Fel76}.
%
This approach is useful to visualise the common factors on the events and develop statistics.
%
Although Schepers took into account the dynamics of the bicycle in his work, these factors do not accurately capture the mechanisms that occur, maintaining the gap on the data needed to understand the dynamics of the event.
%
For example, \cite{Sche12} classifies the effect of a bag on the handlebar as a rider fault, when it can be simplified to a steering perturbation which affects the lateral balance control of the bicycle. &% Maybe this fits better into discussion section.



Then, we find a four-layer approach from \cite{Gil21a}, using direct causes and fall types, which allows to analyse the kinematic form of cyclist's falls.
%
This classification is based on the direct causes, the crash scenario, common factors and the fall type.
%
Direct causes are the same than \cite{Sch12} and \cite{Utr20}, which create the crash scenario depending if it is composed on one or two direct causes.
%
Then, Gildea takes into account the common factors found on the literature.
%
At last, he distinguishes between three fall types: skid-out, pitch-over and topple-over (roll-over, see Figure \ref{fig: flowchart}).
%
Using this methodology, it is possible to cluster the events and compare them with the outcomes of the crash.



\cite{Sch21} aims to develop a crash detection system, which uses measured accelerations on the bicycle to detect the events.
%
To identify events where a crash occurs, he develops a hierarchy based on his definition of stages in a crash, defining four classes: riding state, collision, ground hit and tipping over.
%
All of them have sub-classifications, which combined result in 60 possible events, from which 38 are categorised as crashes.
%
Interestingly, the last layer of this method distinguishes between Nose over (pitch-over), tip over (roll-over) and upright bicycle.
%
Nevertheless, this classification does not provide a detailed description of the crashes, which constitutes part of the future work discussed.



\cite{Gil23a} develops a video-based crash detection system.
%
For this reason, he uses the proposed categorisation of \cite{Gil21a} and identifies the extent of the loss of control.
%
With this, a new level of category is created: minor, substantial and complete loss of control.
%
In minor loss of control, the rider is able to recover the balance and continue riding.
%
Substantial loss of control refers to the situation where the rider loses control and needs to stop or dismount before continuing its ride.
%
Finally, complete loss of control refers to the events where the rider falls to the ground.




In this point, we encounter the lack of information about the dynamics of the accidents \citep{}.
%
Across the studies, the conclusion is that the most frequent causes of SCCs are skidding, loss of control by braking or steering manoeuvres, and external perturbations such as hitting a kerb \citep{Sch12, Her18, Utr20, Alg22}.
%
Moreover, the way of interaction with the environment can increase the risk, e.g. the angle of crossing a tram line \citep{Gil23}.
%
However, there is no information about which one of the wheels slips, longitudinal or lateral velocity, steering angle, braking force, etc.
%
Therefore, it is necessary to provide a classification that highlights this gap and invites to further research.

\subsection{Bicycle dynamics} \label{sec: bike}


Bicycles are human-powered two-wheeled vehicles that require lateral balance control to maintain upright position, which have been a matter of scientific interest for more than 100 years \citep{Car99, Whi99}.
%
The now classical approach for its study considers the bicycle as a dynamical system composed of four rigid bodies: rear frame, front frame, and two wheels.
%
Assuming a rider rigidly attached to the bicycle, \cite{Mei07} presents the linearised equations of motion for the three degree of freedom model.
%
From these linear equations, the stability of the system is studied, showing two main modes of vibration: weave and capsize, which are the particular interest due to its danger to the rider when unstable.
%
Weave is a low-frequency oscillatory motion, from roll and steering, while capsize is a non-oscillatory inverted pendulum-like motion.
%
Thus, the linearised bicycle model was shown to be stable in the range of ${\sim}4.3$ to $6.0 $[\unit{\m\per\s}].
%
Furthermore, if components compliance is included, wobble mode arises \citep{Sha71, Sch13}, which is an oscillatory motion of the steering, also danger when unstable \citep{Lim06}.
%%


Out of its self-stability speed range, human-bicycle systems rely on human control to maintain balance \citep{Koo13}.
%
\cite{Koo11} demonstrated that the basis of bicycles' self-stability relies on mass distribution and steering coupled to lean angle, contrary to the common assumption of wheels' angular momentum main role.
%
Similarly, it is known that most control actions come from the steering, and at low speeds knees start to play a role in the control task \citep{Koo09}.
%
However, the steering is not the only control input, the rider makes use of the pedals and brakes to control longitudinal acceleration and deceleration, respectively.



Thus, the task of riding a bicycle consists in first maintaining balance and then following a desired trajectory \citep{Koo13}.
%
For crashes where we hypothesise that the rider loses control on the vehicle, the interest is on the former, which is achieved by controlling the roll angle of the bicycle through steering inputs.
%
\cite{Hes12} explains this task with a human controller model, where the rider receives feedback from the bicycle through the proprioceptive, vestibular and visual systems.
%
Here, the proprioceptive loop refers to the information received from the body, such as the position of the limbs, and the vestibular system provides information about the orientation of the head and body in space.
%
Hence, the proprioceptive feedback comes from steering angle, the vestibular comes from roll rate and visual feedback comes from roll angle.
%
With this information, the rider is capable of controlling the balance of the system and perform the task of following a trajectory at the same time.

% Insert image here from Hess



Being the steering the main control input and the fastest loop on the model \citep{Hes12}, its role is crucial for riding a bicycle.
%
Hence, if the steering gets locked, the main balance control input disappears, losing the \textit{controllability} of the system \cite{Koo11}.
%
Consequently, disturbances on the steering negatively affect rider's ability to control the bicycle.




In addition, bicycles are sensitive to aerodynamic forces, which are studied as a resultant force acting on the centre of pressure \cite{Gon24}.
%
Aerodynamic forces acting opposite to bicycle motion are known as aerodynamic drag, and its study becomes interesting for enhancing cycling performance \citep{Deb11}.
%
On the other hand, forces acting on the lateral plane, such as cross winds, can affect the steering generating large steering torque depending on front wheel aerodynamics \cite{Mao25}. 
%
For this reason, ...



Concerning bicycles under longitudinal acceleration, their limitations rely on load transfer and tyre grip.
%
On commuter riding conditions, acceleration limit is far to be reached, different to braking limit, which for most bicycles is around $0.5g$ \citep{Bec04}, where $g$ represents the gravitational acceleration.
%
This deceleration can be reached through brake action or collisions on the front wheel, where both cases result in the so called `pitch-over' crashes \citep{Bre10}.




Lastly, bicycles in cornering have a particular behaviour that have been also a matter of interest on its own.
%
Single-track vehicles, such as bicycles and motorcycles, lean into the corner to maintain the equilibrium of forces, see Figure \ref{fig: cornering}.
%
This manoeuvres generate large lateral forces on the tyres, which is supported by friction between the rubber and the ground.
%
Hence, friction on the tyre-ground interface is a complex phenomenon of at least three mechanisms: adhesion, deformation and wear \citep{Han03}.
%
Adhesion refers to the bonding between both surfaces, and deformation is the capability of the tyre to mold into the road irregularities.
%
On the other hand, wear occurs when local stress on the tyre surpasses its elastic limit, causing tearing and dissipating energy, which results in friction.
%
Therefore, when there is an external agent on this interface, such as water, dust or another layer of material, tyre friction relies only on deformation.



Tyre friction exposes a non-linear behaviour on lateral loads, which has a saturation limit.
%
The angle between the direction of the wheel and the direction of motion is known as `slip angle'.
%
When the saturation limit is reached, the tyre loses grip and starts to exhibit a phenomenon called `skidding'. 
%
The extent of loss of grip will lead to different crash configurations, as explained in the next section, which is particularly interesting to understand SCCs.
%


In summary, bicycles can be self-stable but require human control to maintain balance in a wider range of speed.
%
This human control is studied as dependant on the feedback from the two main degrees of freedom of the system.
%
Within the motion of the system, we find longitudinal and lateral dyanmics that face different challenges in their interaction with the environment.
%
Thus, the human-bicycle system is a complex dynamical system that requires a deep understanding of its dynamics to assess its failure mechanisms.

\subsection{Manoeuvre}

The first layer consists on the manoeuvre that the rider is executing at the moment of entering to the critical scenario.
%
Here we understand straight-running as the action of riding a straight path, maintaining balance through minor steering adjustments and no major variations on the states of the system.
%
Longitudinal acceleration manoeuvres refer to braking and acceleration, both performed by the user.
%
Steady and planned cornering refers to the situation where the rider is performing a controlled turning.
%
Low speed makes reference mainly to start and stop riding, where users require major balance actions to maintain the bicycle in upright position.
%
Unplanned turning or avoidance manoeuvre is when the rider executes a sudden evasive turn to avoid an obstacle.
%
Stunting refers to any action that is not intended for riding, such as riding with one hand, no hands, or standing on the pedals, which limits the control of the rider on the bicycle.




\subsection{Excitation of the system}

Due to the complexity of the human-bicycle system, it is subjected to several excitations:
%
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intrinsic (Int):} Makes reference to any excitation that occurs due to the behaviour of the bicycle itself.
        %
        Mainly composed of the modes of vibration.
    \item \textbf{Rider (Rid):} Rider excitations are linked to rider control inputs, if they are incorrect, it leads to a failure mechanism.
    \item \textbf{External (Ext):} This refers to any perturbation, force or change of state exerted by the environment to the system.
\end{itemize}


On the literature, these factors are commonly called as `bicycle', `rider' and `environmental/infrastructure', respectively.

\subsection{Mechanisms of crash}


Currently on the literature, we find several known mechanisms of crashes, normally clustered as skidding, collision with obstacle, falls, braking, drifting off the road, among others.
%
However, these `boxes' do not fit the broad spectrum of mechanisms, nor capture the dynamics involved in the events.
%
For this reason, we propose the following mechanisms based on the literature and observation from a dataset of publicly available videos \cite{}.
%


Since the aim of this classification is to capture the behaviour of the bicycle, there is no difference between the reason why tyre friction reaches its saturation limit.
%
Thus, skidding on wet road, wet leaves, ice or gravel can be simply clustered into tyre friction failure.


\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Modes of vibration:} Oscillatory modes of vibration of the bicycle: weave and wobble.
    \item \textbf{Rider-bicycle joint:} The rider is `attached' to the bicycle in 5 points: handlebar (both hands), pedals and saddle.
        %
        When one or more of these detaches, the rider loses a control input to the system.
    \item \textbf{Balance control:} This makes reference to the ability of the human of balancing the bicycle as an inverted pendulum, moving the steering to control its equilibrium.
        %
        When the control action is not correct, the rider reaches a point where is not possible to maintain the balance.
        %
        An edge-case example are bags hanging on the handlebar, these affect the steering leading to a failure on the balance control.
    \item \textbf{Load transfer:} Longitudinal accelerations generate longitudinal load transfer, which remains unnoticed for most casual riders.
        %
        However, under large accelerations, this load transfer exceeds the maximum tolerable values for maintaining the bicycle in a stable configuratiopn.
    \item \textbf{Tyre friction:} The only connection between the bicycle and the environment is on the tyre-ground contact interface.
        %
        Tyres have a saturation limit for the grip offered, when this limit is reached, they start to skid and the rider loses control of the vehicle.
    \item \textbf{Contact forces:} This makes reference to any collision or force exerted on the bicycle, such as collision with an object, or steering perturbations due to uneven road surfaces.
    \item \textbf{Aerodynamic forces:} Aerodynamics can play an important role when large wind gusts strike the rider by the side, generating a major lateral force, which can destabilise the system.
\end{itemize}

On the literature, these mechanisms and their approaches are treated as absolutes, with no distinction on the application point.
%
For this reason, we include front or rear, along with longitudinal or lateral features to complement the mechanism.

\subsection{Characteristic motion}

Currently on the literature we find three characteristic motions for two-wheeled single-track vehicle crashes \cite{Rose}, see Figure \ref{fig: crashes} for schematic representation of the motions. 

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pitch-over:} The main characteristic of this motion is one of the wheels lifting from the ground, following a trajectory that finishes with the front wheel behind the rear wheel.
    \item \textbf{Low-side:} The human-bicycle system follows an excessive roll rate in the same direction as at the beginning.
    \item \textbf{High-side:} Characterised for a sudden deceleration of the wheel while in lateral motion, which leads to a violent negative roll rate with respect to the direction of the initial motion.
\end{itemize}

However, it is possible that the crash scenario does not provide a characteristic motion of the bicycle.
%
In those cases, it is possible to assess it from the characteristic motion of the rider on the event.
