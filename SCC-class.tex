\section*{Abstract} 

\textbf{
Bicycle usage increases every year and the measures taken to reduce the number of crashes have not been able to accomplish their goal.
%
To prevent and minimise single-cyclist crash risk, it is vital to understand how and why these events occur.
%
Currently, the common factors among these events have been discussed and studied, but the dynamics of the bicycle in a crash scenario are still unclear.
%
In order to promote the seek for this data, it is required a classification of single-cyclist crashes that highlights this gap.
%
Therefore, the aim of this work is to develop a single-cyclist crash classification based on bicycle dynamics.
%
The proposed classification works under the multi-layer principle, allowing the combination of factors to respond to modern crash model requirements.
%
The resulting categories are manoeuvre, excitation of the system, crash mechanism and characteristic motion.
%
Along with sub-categories for rider control, signal input and location of the fault mechanism.
%
This work provides a new approach to classifying bicycle crashes and clarifies commonly used concepts in the field.
}

\subsection{Terminology} \label{subsec: keyconcepts}

% Include definition of dynamics or at least how it is going to be used here.

Bicycle crashes that do not involve multiple entities are given a variety of names in the literature: single-bicycle crash, single-actor bicycle crash \citep{Ali23}, single-cyclist crash, among others (see Table \ref{tab: results}).
%
On one hand, the expression single-bicycle crash, even being the most common, as seen in Table \ref{tab: results}, neglects the rider, making reference to a problem only oriented on the bicycle.
%
On the other hand, single-actor bicycle crashes assumes that there are no other actors involved in the event, which neglects indirect effects of other road users, e.g. a pedestrian entering into the bike lane triggering an avoidance manoeuvre that ends in a crash.
%
Moreover, any other denomination that includes the word `accident' neglects the preventable nature of these events \citep{Dav01}.
%
Therefore, for this work we adopt single-cyclist crash, due to its reference to the rider and the bicycle as the main actors in the event, allowing other indirect actors.
%%



% The bicycle rider system consists of a simplified human modelled as a multi-segment body attached to a bicycle.
% %
% The bicycle is composed by front and rear assemblies, where the front assembly includes handlebar, fork, and brake, and the rear assembly encompasses

% - The rider is attached to the bicycle.
% - The rider is simplified to a multi segment body.
% - The rider acts as a controller of the system.
% - The bicycle is composed by front and rear assemblies.
% - Front assembly encompasses fork, handlebar, front wheel and brake.
% -  The rear assembly is the frame, rear wheel, transmission and saddle.

The rider bicycle system refers to the closed loop in which a human is riding a bicycle \citep{Hor95}.
%

During this article, we will refer to the bicycle and the human coupled together in motion as a dynamical system, which is modelled by non-linear differential equations.
%
Assuming the rider as a controller of the system, we identify the existence of \textit{controllability}, which means that all system variables can be influenced by the input \citep{Sef01, Ver12}.
%

The rider controls the lateral balance of the bicycle mainly through the steering, and also with the contribution of hips, pedal pressure balance, and legs.

For longitudinal control, the rider relies on pedal power input and brakes.

In this case, the control input from the human to the bicycle is the steering.



A perturbation will be understood by its dictionary definition as any variation in the system that causes it to change its regular movement \citep{Cam25}.
%
An active perturbation is a controlled force from an external source, such as other vehicle colliding with the system.
%
% A passive perturbation is a non-controlled force, e.g. the force exerted by road irregularities on bicycle tyres.
% %%

`An accident is an undesired and unplanned event that results in a loss, damage or injury' \citep{Wie18}. 


We will define a crash to be sudden and unintended event that results in a disruption of the normal and controlled motion of the human-bicycle system.
%
In the analysis of vehicular collisions, there are three phases: pre-impact motion, impact motion and post-impact motion \citep{Par01}.
%
Translating this approach to bicycle crashes, pre-crash motion encompasses the time window from the perturbation to the moment where the any possible control action of the rider will end in a crash.
%
Then, the crash stage occurs, until the moment when the system stops its motion.
%
Post-crash stage is limited in single-cyclist crashes and can be assessed as the moment when the vehicle and the rider are disconnected and presenting independent motions \citep{Llo16}.
%
Figures \ref{fig: highside}, \ref{fig: lowside} and \ref{fig: pitchover} show the schematics of the main crash configurations in these three phases.



A common factor in bicycle crashes are events caused by failure in the adherence between the tyre and the road.
%
Tyre-ground interaction is a complex phenomenon later discussed in this work, however, we find different concepts, which although similar, are different and can lead to confusion.
%
Here, we find the concepts of `skid', `slide', and `slip'.
%
The term `slip' refers to the ratio of the lateral and the forward velocity of the wheel, and constitutes one of the main characteristics of tyres \citep{Pac06}.
%
Therefore, it should not be used to refer to the excessive lateral motion of the tyres as a result of reaching its saturation limit, i.e. when the tyre can not handle more lateral load and the friction force reaches its limit.
%
`Slide' means when all parts of the tire contact patch have a relative motion with respect to the road surface, which is a severe case of slip, resulting in other phenomenon.
%
Therefore, the appropriate concept to describe the loss of control due to the lack of grip on the tyre-ground interface is `skidding' \citep{Kar13}, which is a term specific for vehicles  \citep{Cam25}.

Both vehicles experience similar crash configurations \citep{Haw13, Ken22, Llo16}, including tyre skidding, inability to control the system through the steering, excessive pitch rotation, among others later discussed in this paper.

\subsection{Single-cyclist crash anatomy} \label{subsec: crash}


In the most basic understanding of a single-cyclist crash, this event is the result of the rider losing control of the vehicle, this is, not being able to maintain stability or follow the desired direction.
%
\cite{Ful05} describes in his model the loss of control as a relation between task demand and rider capability, when the task demand surpasses rider capability, the rider loses control and the crash occurs.
%
This model considers physical characteristics and knowledge of the driver on what to do under determined circumstances to define the boundaries its capability.
%
However, this capacity is also affected by other factors related to the circumstance, such as awareness, emotion, fatigue, among others.
%
On the other hand, task demanding elements encompass travelling speed, environmental factors, such as road characteristics and other road users.
%
Of these factors, the driver has direct control of the travelling speed, achieving balance between driver capacity and its awareness to the task.
%
While car drivers can reduce task demand by decreasing speed, this is not always the case for bicycle riders, due to the increase of user stabilisation requirements at low speeds \citep{Koo11, Sch13, Vla15}.
%
In general, the theory shows an almost constant driver capability balancing with changing task demands, which should not surpass the capability threshold.
%%



Now, when task demands surpasses rider's capability, a critical scenario is triggered, which translates into a loss of control and a possible crash, depending on the extent of the loss of control \citep{Gil23}.
%
Factors that increase task demand and contribute to critical scenarios are known in the literature.
%
As seen in Table \ref{tab: results}, the most repeated events are skidding, low speed riding, braking, `loss of control', and collision with an object.
%
These events require a precise and quick response from the rider, which is can be assumed as a high-demand task in Fullner's model.


\subsubsection{Known mechanisms}

Here we list and provide a short definition of the crash mechanisms found on the literature.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Skidding:} one or both tyres loss grip. \\
    {\footnotesize \citep{Alg22, Bec19, Her18, Ohl19, Sch12, Utr20}}
    \item \textbf{Braking mistakes:} brake over actuation can produce wheel lock, which can end in a pitch-over crash or losing grip in one of the tyres. \\
    {\footnotesize \citep{Alg22, Gil21, Her18, Ohl19, Sch12}}
    \item \textbf{Collision:} collision with objects on the road, these can be objects temporarily or permanently placed there. \\
    {\footnotesize \citep{Bec19, De14, Gil21, Her18}}
    \item \textbf{Loss of control on a descent:} no further definition. \\
    {\footnotesize \citep{Bec19}}
    \item \textbf{Riding off the road:} the rider is unable to maintain the desired trajectory on the road, which results with the bicycle reaching the edge and colliding with the kerb or falling on the non-road area. \\
    {\footnotesize \citep{Gil21, Her18, Sch12, Utr20}}
    \item \textbf{Uneven road:} uneven road surfaces such as cobblestones or improperly maintained roads, cause the wheels to loose contact with the ground. \\
    {\footnotesize \citep{Alg22, Gil21, Sch12}}
    \item \textbf{Getting on/off the bicycle:} users starting the ride or stopping, this involves the manoeuvre from standing to sit on the bicycle at low speeds, which increases balance task demand and the rider places its weight mainly on the pedals. \\
    {\footnotesize \citep{Alg22, Boe17, Ohl19, Utr20}}
    \item \textbf{Mechanical malfunction:} any mechanical failure from the bicycle, non working brakes, broken chain, among others. \\
    {\footnotesize \citep{Alg22, Bec19, Gil21, Ohl19, Sch12}}
    \item \textbf{Caught in track:} refers to one of the wheels falling on the gap of tram tracks, which locks the steering if the front wheel is locked. \\
    {\footnotesize \citep{Alg22}}
    \item \textbf{Object on the road:} discussed as a type of collision. \\
    {\footnotesize \citep{Alg22, Bec19, Boe17, Gil21, Her18, Sch12}}
    \item \textbf{Evasive manoeuvre:} the rider executes a manoeuvre to avoid an obstacle and loses control. \\
    {\footnotesize \citep{Alg22, De14, Gil21, Her18, Ohl19, Sch12, Utr20}}
    \item \textbf{Tram track interaction:} the two mechanisms discussed for tram track interaction are one of the wheels getting locked, or losing grip due to the different friction coefficient of track surface. \\
    {\footnotesize \citep{Bec19, Her18, Ohl19}}
    \item \textbf{Striking pothole:} when the wheel strikes a pothole, the contact point changes, diminishing the available grip, or causing an excessive deceleration. \\
    {\footnotesize \citep{Bec19}}
    \item \textbf{Kerb hit:} when the bicycle reaches the edge of the road, the wheels can collide with the kerb, exerting an unexpected force on the system. \\
    {\footnotesize \citep{Boe17, Gil21, Sch12, Utr20}}
    \item \textbf{Road surface conditions:} uneven roads or slippery surfaces such as wet leaves, gravel, ice or water on the road. \\
    {\footnotesize \citep{Gil21, Her18}}
    \item \textbf{Front wheel hit:} the front wheel hits the curb or an object such as a bag hanging from the handlebar can hit it. \\
    {\footnotesize \citep{Gil21, Sch12, Utr20}}
    \item \textbf{Turning mistake:} \\
    {\footnotesize \citep{Gil21}}
    \item \textbf{Stunt:} refers to doing stunts where one of the wheels lifts from the ground, riding without one or both hands. \\
    {\footnotesize \citep{Gil21, Sch12}}
    \item \textbf{Unable to keep balance:} the rider is not capable of maintaining the bicycle in upright position. \\
    {\footnotesize \citep{Her18}}
    \item \textbf{Crossing a threshold:} the wheel hits a threshold and produces a mechanisms similar to tram track interaction or pothole striking. \\
    {\footnotesize \citep{Her18}}
    \item \textbf{Slipping off pedals:} when the rider is exerting a high-power input into the system, if the foot-pedal joint fails, the rider suddenly changes its load distribution with the bicycle. \\
    {\footnotesize \citep{Her18, Utr20}}
    \item \textbf{Low speed loss of balance:} at low speeds, bicycles have higher balance requirements from the user, when the rider is not capable of controlling the steering properly, it produces a crash. \\
    {\footnotesize \citep{Ohl19, Sch12}}
    \item \textbf{Something caught on the wheel:} objects getting into one of the wheels suddenly locking it. \\
    {\footnotesize \citep{Alg22, Ohl19}}
    \item \textbf{Fall:} no further definition on the literature. \\
    {\footnotesize \citep{Utr20}}
    %\item \textbf{Other: } %\citep{Alg22, Bec19, Gil21, Her18, Sch12}
\end{enumerate}

%
Here, Schepers reviews the existent description of single-cyclist crashes, along with the basic concepts to understand bicycle dynamics.
%
Then, he divides the three components needed for a crash: rider, bicycle, environment, and includes the category `other', analogous to the analysis for car crashes made by \cite{Fel76}.
%
He revises the known crash circumstances and factors, such as age, amount of bicycle usage, road surface, type of bicycle, speed, among others.
%
The data collected came mainly from surveys sent to people that was in the hospital due to a bicycle crash within the two prior months.
%
The results of the classification based on survey responses showed 85\% of agreement between two researchers.
%
It concludes that single-cyclist crashes can be categorized according to direct causes, and for future work, recommends to research specific crash types.




Then, we find a four-layer approach from \cite{Gil21a}, using direct causes and fall types, which allows to analyse the kinematic form of cyclist's falls.
%
This classification is based on the direct causes, the crash scenario, common factors and the fall type.
%
Direct causes are the same as \cite{Sch12} and \cite{Utr20}, which create the crash scenario depending if it is composed on one or two direct causes.
%
Then, Gildea takes into account the common factors found on the literature.
%
At last, he distinguishes between three fall types: pitch-over, topple-over and skid-out.
%
Where pitch-over refers to a sudden deceleration causing the cyclist to be projected forwards due to inertia.
%
Topple-over is the cyclist falling to the side, and skid-out is when the tyre loses traction and the bicycle falls beneath the rider. 
%
Using this methodology, it is possible to cluster the events and compare them with the outcomes of the crash.



\cite{Sch21} aims to develop a crash detection system, which uses measured accelerations on the bicycle to detect the events.
%
To identify events where a crash occurs, he develops a hierarchy based on his definition of stages in a crash, defining four classes: riding state, collision, ground hit and tipping over.

All of them have sub-states, which combined result in 60 possible events, from which 38 are categorised as crashes.
%
Interestingly, the last layer of this method distinguishes between Nose over (pitch-over), tip over (roll-over) and upright bicycle.

Nevertheless, this classification does not provide a detailed description of the crashes, which constitutes part of the future work discussed.



\cite{Gil23a} develops a video-based crash detection system.

\subsection{Bicycle dynamics} \label{sec: bike}

% Define here what are or what is bicycle dynamics.

Bicycle dynamics refers to the study of the motion and stability of bicycles, governed by nonlinear, coupled equations of motion that describe the interactions between the bicycle, tire-ground contact interaction, steering, geometry, and rider inputs under various operating conditions.

Bicycle dynamics is the study of bicycle motion and stability governed by nonlinear, coupled equations involving steering, tire forces, and rider interaction.

This is important because it highlights the failure mechanisms within the bicycle-rider system that end in a crash.


Bicycles are human-powered two-wheeled vehicles that require lateral balance control to maintain an upright position, which have been a matter of scientific interest for more than 100 years \citep{Car99, Whi99}.
%
The now-classical approach for its study considers the bicycle as a dynamical system composed of four rigid bodies: rear frame, front frame, and two wheels.
%
Assuming a rider rigidly attached to the bicycle, flat ground, non-slipping tyres and permanent contact with the ground, \cite{Mei07} presents the linearised equations of motion for the three-degree-of-freedom model.
%
These degrees of freedom are roll $\varphi$, steering $\delta$ and rear wheel rotation $\omega_r$ (see Figure \ref{fig: dof}.



From the linear equations of motion, the stability of the system is studied, showing a self-stable behaviour and three modes of vibration: weave, capsize, and caster.
%
Weave is a low-frequency oscillatory motion involving coupled roll and steer; capsize is a non-oscillatory motion similar to an inverted pendulum, and caster is the self-aligning motion of the front wheel.
%
From these three modes, weave and capsize are particularly interesting for their danger of loss of control when unstable.
%
Furthermore, if components compliance is included, wobble mode arises \citep{Sha71, Sch13}, which is an oscillatory motion of the steering, also dangerous when unstable \citep{Lim06}.
%%


Regarding the foundations of bicycles' self-stability, \cite{Koo11} demonstrated that it relies on mass distribution and steering coupled to lean angle.
%
Out of its self-stability speed range, bicycles rely on human control to maintain upright stable balance \citep{Koo13}.
%
To this end, the rider turns the steering into the falling side, which moves the contact point of the front wheel, shifting the equilibrium of the system.
%
Although most control actions come from the steering, at low speeds, knees start to play a role in the control task \citep{Koo09}.
%
However, the steering is not the only control input, the rider makes use of the pedals and brakes to control longitudinal acceleration and deceleration, respectively.


%
Thus, the task of riding a bicycle consists in first maintaining balance and then following a desired trajectory.
%
For crashes where we hypothesise that the rider loses control on the vehicle, the interest is on the former, which is achieved by controlling the roll angle of the bicycle through steering inputs.
%
\cite{Hes12} explains this task with a human controller model, where the rider receives feedback from the bicycle through the proprioceptive, vestibular and visual systems.
%
Here, the proprioceptive loop refers to the information received from the body, such as the position of the limbs, and the vestibular system provides information about the orientation of the head and body in space.
%
Hence, the proprioceptive feedback comes from steering angle, the vestibular comes from roll rate and visual feedback comes from roll angle.
%
With this information, the rider is capable of controlling the balance of the system and perform the task of following a trajectory at the same time.

% Insert image here from Hess



Being that steering is the main control input and the fastest loop on the model of \cite{Hes12}, its role is crucial for riding a bicycle.
%
Hence, if the steering gets locked, the main balance control input disappears, losing the controllability of the system \cite{Koo11}.
%
Consequently, disturbances to the steering negatively affect the rider's ability to control the bicycle.
%%


In addition, bicycles are sensitive to aerodynamic forces, which are studied as a resultant force acting on the centre of pressure.
%
Aerodynamic forces acting opposite to bicycle motion are known as aerodynamic drag, and its study becomes interesting for enhancing cycling performance \citep{Deb11}.
%
On the other hand, forces acting on the lateral plane, such as cross winds, can affect the steering, generating large steering torque depending on front wheel aerodynamics \cite{Mao25}. 
%
For this reason, it is important to take them into account as a possible disturbance on the system in certain conditions.
%%


Concerning bicycles under longitudinal acceleration, their limitations rely on load transfer and tyre grip \citep{Lot21}.
%
On commuter riding conditions, the acceleration limit is far from being reached, different to the deceleration limit, which is subject to several factors.
%
For most bicycles, this limit is around $ 0.5 g$ \citep{Bec04}, where $g$ is the gravitational acceleration.
%
Deceleration limit can be reached through brake actuation or collisions on the front wheel, where both cases result in the so-called `pitch-over' crashes \citep{Bre10}.
%
If tyre friction limit is reached, the rotational dynamics of the wheels change and the control of the vehicle becomes limited.
%%


Furthermore, tyre friction also plays a major role on bicycle cornering dynamics.

% Lastly, bicycles in cornering have a particular behaviour that has also been a matter of interest in its own.
%
Single-track vehicles, such as bicycles and motorcycles, lean into the corner to maintain the equilibrium of forces, see Figure \ref{fig: cornering}.
%
These manoeuvres generate large lateral forces on the tyres, which are supported by friction between the rubber and the ground.
%
Hence, friction on the tyre-ground interface is a complex phenomenon of at least three mechanisms: adhesion, deformation and wear \citep{Han03}.
%
Adhesion refers to the bonding between both surfaces, and deformation is the capability of the tyre to adapt to the road irregularities.
%
On the other hand, wear occurs when local stress on the tyre surpasses its elastic limit, causing tearing and dissipating energy, which results in friction.
%
Therefore, when there is an external agent on this interface, such as water, dust or another layer of material, tyre friction relies only on deformation \citep{Mic01}.



Tyre friction exposes a non-linear behaviour on lateral loads, which has a saturation limit at a given force.
%
When the saturation limit is reached, the tyre loses grip and starts to exhibit a phenomenon called `skidding'. 
%
Within the operating range of a tyre, the angle between the direction of the wheel and the direction of motion is known as `slip angle'.
%
The extent of loss of grip will lead to different crash configurations, as explained in the next section, which is particularly interesting to understand single-cyclist crashes.
%

    \caption{Steady turning: roll angle of the motorcycle equipped with zero thickness tires. From \cite{Cos06}.}
    \label{fig: cornering}
\end{figure}

In summary, bicycles can be self-stable but require human control to maintain balance over a wider range of speeds.
%
This human control is studied as dependent on the feedback from the two main degrees of freedom of the system.
%
Within the motion of the system, we find longitudinal and lateral dynamics that face different challenges in their interaction with the environment.
%
Thus, the human-bicycle system is a complex dynamical system that requires a deep understanding of its dynamics to assess its failure mechanisms.

When the cause of accidents is assessed as a series of discrete events in a specific time, it is called `Linear chain-of-failure event model'.
%
These models graphically show the series of events that lead to a crash, however, they often ignore secondary potential factors \citep{Zha22}.
%
Therefore, towards the aim of `zero fatalities' \citep{Men17}, new models are required, which allow the combination of factors to provide a more integral analysis \citep{Zha22}.
%
Aligned with this idea, we propose a multi-layer classification, which allows to combine the influence of different factors on the outcome of the event.
%
The classification consists of four main layers and three sub-layers, as seen in Figure \ref{fig: flowchart}.
%
The main layers are composed of already known concepts in the field, such as manoeuvres, excitation of the system, mechanisms of crash, and motion of the system during the crash.
%
On the other hand, sub-layers expose gaps found in the literature, such as human control in crash or pre-crash scenarios, characteristics of the excitation of the system and the location of the mechanism.


The multi-layer approach allows to classify the crash event with different depth levels according to the available information.
%
Thus, this gives a flexible base that can be applied in different fields, depending on the objective sought.
%


The way of understanding the diagram on Figure \ref{fig: flowchart} goes:
%
The rider is executing a manoeuvre, then, the system experiences an excitation that leads to a critical situation.
%
Depending on the human control and the characteristics of the signal, the task demand increases above rider capability limit and one or more crash mechanisms appear.
%
From the crash mechanisms, we can identify its main location, which leads to one of the characteristic motions, to finally end in a crash.
%
For instance, the rider is doing a normal speed turn to the right ($\mid 5$[m/s]), the front wheel steps on a low-grip zone of the road surface (external excitation), the main failure mechanism is the tyre friction.
%
As the front wheel loses grip, the rotational dynamics change and it produces an understeering behaviour, from which the rider can not retake control of the balance through steering inputs, leading to an excessive roll rate (low-side crash).
%
Here, would be interesting to know what is the available time between grip decrease and the first response from the rider, or how the loss of grip acts on the system as a signal.


\subsection{Manoeuvre}

The first layer consists of the manoeuvre that the rider is executing at the moment of entering the critical scenario.
%
Here we understand straight-running as the action of riding a straight path, maintaining balance through minor steering adjustments and no major variations on the states of the system.
%
Longitudinal acceleration manoeuvres refer to braking and acceleration, both performed by the user.
%
Steady and planned cornering refers to the situation where the rider is performing a controlled turn, with a clear trajectory in mind.
%
Low speed makes reference mainly to starting and stopping riding, where users require major balance actions to maintain the bicycle in an upright position.
%
An unplanned turning or avoidance manoeuvre is when the rider executes a sudden evasive turn to avoid an obstacle.
%
Stunting refers to any action that is not intended for riding, such as riding with one hand, no hands, or standing on the pedals, which changes and limits the control of the rider on the bicycle.



\subsection{Excitation of the system}

Due to the complexity of the human-bicycle system, it is subjected to several excitations, which we collect in three classes.
%
These classes are aligned with \cite{Fel76}, where a crash is a chain of events involving a rider, a vehicle and an environmental factor.
%
Similarly, \citep{Sch12} refers to these three categories as direct causes of the crash.
%
In this work, we refer to them as rider, intrinsic and external, respectively.
%
The description for each of them is:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intrinsic:} Makes reference to any excitation that occurs due to the behaviour of the bicycle itself.
        %
        This encompasses the modes of vibration and bicycle malfunctions.
    \item \textbf{Rider:} Refers when the major excitation of the system comes from a control action from the rider.
    %
    When the control is incorrect, it leads to a failure mechanism.
    \item \textbf{External:} This refers to any perturbation, force or change of state exerted by the environment on the system.
\end{itemize}



\subsection{Mechanisms of crash}

Currently, in the literature, we find several known mechanisms of crashes, normally clustered as skidding, collision with an obstacle, falls, braking, and drifting off the road, among others.
%
However, these `boxes' do not fit the broad spectrum of mechanisms, nor capture the dynamics involved in the events.
%
For this reason, we propose the following mechanisms based on the literature and observation from the video dataset:
%
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Modes of vibration:} Oscillatory modes of vibration of the bicycle: weave and wobble, creating an unpredictable trajectory of the bicycle.
    \item \textbf{Rider-bicycle joint:} The rider is `attached' to the bicycle in five points: handlebar (both hands), pedals and saddle.
        %
        % When one or more of these detaches, the rider loses a control input to the system.
        %
        When one or more of these detaches, the control from the rider changes and it is prone to fail.
    \item \textbf{Balance control:} This makes reference to the ability of the human to balance the bicycle as an inverted pendulum, moving the steering to control its equilibrium.
        %
        When the control action is not correct, the rider reaches a point where it is not possible to maintain the balance.
    \item \textbf{Load transfer:} Longitudinal accelerations generate longitudinal load transfer, which remains unnoticed for most casual riders.
        %
        However, under large accelerations, these load transfer can easily exceed the maximum tolerable values for maintaining the bicycle in a longitudinally stable configuration.
        %
        Therefore, due to the inertia of the system, a rotational motion is produced, which changes the states of the system out of its boundaries of human control.
    \item \textbf{Tyre friction:} The only connection between the bicycle and the environment is on the tyre-ground contact interface.
        %
        Tyres have a saturation limit for the grip offered, when this limit is reached, they start to skid and the rider loses control of the vehicle.
        %
        Depending on which of the tyres loses grip, the dynamics will change.
        %
        When the rear tyre reaches its saturation limit, the vehicle presents oversteering behaviour.
        %
        Alternatively, when the front wheel loses grip, the vehicle tends to understeer.
    \item \textbf{Contact forces:} This makes reference to any collision or force exerted on the bicycle, such as collision with an object, or steering perturbations.
    \item \textbf{Aerodynamic forces:} Aerodynamics can play an important role when large wind gusts strike the rider by the side, generating a major lateral force, which can destabilise the system.
\end{itemize}

In the literature, these mechanisms and their approaches are treated as absolutes, with no distinction on the application point.
%
For this reason, we include front or rear, along with longitudinal, lateral or vertical as features to further explain the mechanism.

Currently on the literature we find three characteristic motions for two-wheeled single-track vehicle crashes \citep{Bed16, Ros17}, see Figures \ref{fig: highside}, \ref{fig: lowside} and \ref{fig: pitchover} for schematic representation of the motions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pitch-over:} The main characteristic of this motion is one of the wheels lifting from the ground, following a trajectory that finishes with the front wheel behind the rear wheel \citep{Bre10}.
    \item \textbf{Low-side:} The human-bicycle system follows an excessive roll rate in the same direction as at the beginning.
    %
    \cite{Cos07} explains it as a yaw movement that causes over-steering rotation.
    \item \textbf{High-side:} Characterised for a sudden deceleration of the wheel while in lateral motion, which leads to a violent negative roll rate with respect to the direction of the initial motion.

    \cite{Cos07} adds the characteristic of rider upward ejection as a result, frequently occurred in racing motorcycle crashes.
    \item \textbf{No characteristic motion:} This sub-class refers to crashes where the final motion is not clear, or the main event is a collision that stops the motion.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion} \label{sec: discussion}

% Context

In this work, we reviewed the literature on single-cyclist crash classifications, factors, and mechanisms.
%
We found a gap in how existing classifications capture the dynamics of the bicycle and its interaction with the environment.
%
In addition, many commonly used terms are not clarified in all studies.
%
Therefore, we presented the key concepts used in this work, reviewed the existing information and proposed a new classification.


% Bicycle dynamics
Based on bicycle dynamics theory, we were able to capture the possible failure mechanisms that lead to crashes.
%
Particularly, we highlight the modes of vibration as a mechanism that overcomes the control capability of the rider.
%
Similarly, assessing the tyre-ground friction as a general concept encompasses the crashes due to slippery surfaces and road irregularities.
%
Moreover, this mechanism explains the cases where cyclists veer off the road during a corner due to reaching the maximum available friction.
%

% Contrast with existing classifications

The results from the questionnaire showed that our classification has enough scope to avoid responses as `other' or `unknown'.
%
The main categories used for the nature of the excitation are in agreement with \cite{Sch12} and \cite{Fel76}.
%
However, on the sub-categories of causes, here referred as mechanisms, we find major differences \citep{Sch12}.
%
Although there are some similarities, such as identifying collisions, braking mistakes, bicycle malfunction or steering disturbances, the first difference comes from the use of combinations of different mechanisms to explain a single event.
%
With this method, we rank the mechanisms identified according to their hypothesised share of the outcome.



Although \cite{Sch12} took into account the dynamics of the bicycle in his work, these factors do not accurately capture the mechanisms that occur.
%
For example, he classifies the effect of a bag on the handlebar as a rider fault, when it can be simplified to a steering perturbation which affects the lateral balance control of the bicycle. 
%
In general, we find categories narrowed to specific events, which show the effects of infrastructure failures.
%
However, the information obtained is scarce if the interest is on recreating a certain crash scenario, missing the intended manoeuvre by the rider, or the motion of the system during the crash.


Alternatively, \cite{Gil21a} includes the fall type in his classification.
%
Compared to Gildea's classification, we agree on the pitch-over and roll-over motions, subdividing the latter into two specific crash motions.
%
However, the fall motion presented as `skid-out' may represent a skidding mechanism under large lateral loads, which results in a crash by colliding with the kerb or running off the road.
%
Therefore, we differ in defining skidding as a crash motion, since it can be explained through a low-side roll-over motion.

\subsection{Mechanisms}

One important part of our work is providing a detailed description of the hypothesised crash mechanisms.
%
From the mechanisms present in our classification, only `tyre friction' and `contact forces' are explicitly discussed in the literature \citep{Alg22, Bec16, Boe17, Her18, Sch12}.
%
However, we prefer a broader approach where similar mechanisms belong to the same class, e.g. skidding on wet, icy or leaf-covered pavement, all of them represent a loss of tyre friction.
%
Furthermore, crashes involving collision with objects, temporal or permanent, are clustered as contact forces, which also applies for wheels getting stuck on tram tracks.
%
Similarly, low-speed `loss of control' crashes are explained through lateral and longitudinal balance control mechanisms.
%

Using this approach, we avoid classes that are not descriptive of the event, such as `other', `unknown' or `loss of control'.
%
Particularly, based on the crash model from \cite{Ful05}, describing a crash mechanism as `loss of control' becomes redundant, since all crashes occur due to loss of control.
%

\subsection{Questionnaire results}

The questionnaire used to test the scope of this work showed a high percentage of agreement on the mechanisms and motions detected.
%
Nevertheless, the remaining percentage could be explained by the following quote \textit{`Crash cause is similar to beauty: it is in the eye of the beholder'} \citep{Shi19}.
%
In that work, Shinar discusses how crash analysis can vary from different authors regarding what is their main focus.
%
His research found that when police analyse crash data, the common conclusion is an operator fault, due to their focus on law enforcement.
%
Alternatively, when data is analysed by road engineers, they concluded that road defects contributed to 76\% of crashes.
%
Therefore, the classification and analysis are closely related to the intention of the study and highly dependent on the vision of the person who performs the classification.
%
Especially if crashes are being classified from low quality videos that do not provide clear details.
%

% Added value

Altogether, this work contributes to the literature by providing a description of commonly used concepts that are not properly described in current articles in the field.
%
Additionally, we formulate a multi-layer and flexible classification that provides more information about the crashes and clusters them according to similar dynamic characteristics.
%
Moreover, this work highlights the necessity of human motion and control data to further understand the role of the rider in critical scenarios.
%
Similarly, we show that kinematic data is needed to explain how and why the hypothesised mechanisms act.
%


In summary, we revised the existing literature about single-cyclist crashes.
%
We found different classification methods and known mechanisms, however, none of them were focused on bicycle dynamics.
%
In addition, we revise the theory of bicycle dynamics to explain the known mechanisms and provide a more accurate theoretical description.
%
With this, we propose a multi-layer classification that encompasses already known information, along with gaps in the field.


\section{Conclusion} \label{sec: conclusion}

Bicycle safety is a major concern if a modern society looks forward to a green and sustainable mobility.
%
In particular, single-cyclist crashes are increasing, and the mechanisms that cause them are still not clear, slowing down their countermeasures.
%
In this work, we proposed a new classification based on bicycle dynamics that highlights the gaps in the knowledge in order to better understand these events.
%
Future work could delve into the limits of human control of the bicycle in critical scenarios, with the aim of understanding when a crash is going to happen.
